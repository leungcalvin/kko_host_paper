\begin{abstract}
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) at $\lesssim 400$ Mpc provide robust constraints on the contents of the circumgalactic medium of $L^*$ galaxies via the inferred host component of the dispersion measure (DM) budget. 
We study the relationship between FRB host stellar masses \mstar\ and the dispersion measures \dmhm\ left after subtracting the average cosmic contribution out to the redshift of the host. We use the resulting $M^*-\dmh$ relation to constrain models for feedback in isolated galaxies as implemented in the CAMELS suite of hydrodynamical simulations.
We use a sample of 22 FRBs with robust host associations selected from publicly-available FRBs to minimize all contributions to \dmh\ except for that from their circumgalactic medium.
Considering only the three fiducial models available within CAMELS, our results are in statistical tension with Astrid when compared to either IllustrisTNG or SIMBA when interpreted as an upper limit on the host CGM contribution. 
We show that this conclusion is robust to a wide range of assumptions, such as the offset distribution of FRBs from their hosts, the relative rates of FRBs in e.g. star-forming versus massive galaxies, 
Our results directly probe baryonic feedback in star-forming galaxies in the Local Universe $(z < 0.2)$, and indirectly imply a lower limit on the strength of feedback as quantified by the suppression of the matter power spectrum due to baryonic feedback.
This reaches halo masses that are inaccessible by X-ray and Sunyaev-Zeldovich CMB distortions, demonstrating the unique complementarity of FRBs to other gas probes.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Long before the first fast radio burst (FRB) was pinpointed to a cosmologically-distant host galaxy~\citep{chatterjee2017direct,tendulkar2017host},~\citet{mcquinn2014locating} realized the immense potential of FRBs as probes of the cosmic baryons.
The dispersion measure, or DM, of FRBs can precisely measure the column density of electrons between the observer and the FRB source. 
This makes FRBs excellent probes of baryons, sensitive to both the baryons in the cosmic web and the collapsed gas overdensities on the outskirts of galaxies and their dark matter halos.

Since then, two classes of methodologies have emerged that use FRBs to probe extragalactic baryons: one using the relation of the mean extragalactic DM versus redshift, and a second using the variations in the mean.
Using observations from the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP),~\citet{macquart2020census} used the mean extragalactic DM \avgdmcosmic as a function of redshift to locate the so-called ``missing'' baryons to the intergalactic medium (IGM) and constrain the strength of baryonic feedback along each line of sight using an analytical model of the statistics of intercepted halos~\citep{mcquinn2014locating,james2021z}.
That result established that the cosmic contribution to the DM budget, comprised of diffuse IGM baryons and the collapsed baryons in the intervening halos, collectively dominate the total DM for FRB sources beyond $z \sim 0.2$. 

An implied conclusion of~\citet{macquart2020census}, whose eponymous relation now extends to $z =  1.3$~\citep{connor2024gas}, is that the remaining contributions, such as those from the host galaxy, are subdominant to the cosmic components beyond $z \gtrsim 0.5$.
However, the fluctuations in the extragalactic DM after removing \avgdmcosmic\ contain their own wealth of information.
Historically, this residual has been referred to as the ``host contribution'' to the DM budget and is denoted \dmh. It may be estimated by subtracting estimates for the the Galaxy (\dmmw=\dmmwcgm + \dmmwism). In addition, it requires an estimate of the contribution from the cosmic web: calculated either from auxiliary observations~\citep{simha2020disentangling} along that sightline or simply based on the mean cosmic contribution out to the redshift of the host
(\avgdmcosmic=$\dm_\mathrm{intervening~halos} + \dm_\mathrm{IGM}$).
Using the observed FRB dispersion measure \dmtot, we take the latter approach, defining %We adopt the convention of~\citet{bernalescortes2025empirical}, defining
%is defined as

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{\dmhm}{1 + z} = \dmtot -
\avgdmcosmic - \dmmw.
\label{eq:dmh_definition}
\end{equation}

\dmhm includes contributions which can be further decomposed in the rest frame of the host galaxy:
\begin{equation}
    \dmhm = \dmhcgm + \dmhism + \dmhcbm + \dm_{LSS}.
    \label{eq:dmh_contributions}
\end{equation}

For each observed sightline, these correspond to the CGM of the host galaxy at the endpoint of each FRB~\citep{prochaska2019probing,medlock2024probing}, the host-galactic ISM~\citep{cassanelli2024fast}, contributions from the stellar ``circum-burst'' environment of the FRB~\citep[see e.g.][]{tendulkar2017host, niu2021repeating,ocker2022large,caleb2023subarc}, individual groups and clusters~\citep{lee2023frb,connor2023deep}, and the large-scale structure along the line of sight~\citep[see e.g.][]{khrykin2024flimflam,medlock2025constraining}. 

In particular, if the circumgalactic contribution can be isolated from the others, it could play a crucial role in understanding the three-way relationship between the stellar populations of galaxies, their circumgalactic gas environments, and their dark matter halos. 
Understanding the baryon cycle as a function of stellar mass ($M*$) and halo mass ($M_h$) is a major goal of galaxy formation and cosmology, and has implications for nascent weak lensing surveys, whose constraining power hinges on an accurate and precise understanding of the impact of baryonic feedback on the matter power spectrum~\citep[for example]{white2004baryons, zhan2004effect, rudd2008effects}.

Existing efforts to constrain sub-grid models of baryonic feedback using observational gas probes have focused on X-ray and thermal and kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich (tSZ and kSZ) observations, which are readily observable only in $M_h \sim 10^{13-15}$ halos~\citep[e.g.][]{nagai2007effects,tanimura2021direct,battaglia2016tau,hadzhiyska2023interpreting,schaan2021atacama,hadzhiyska2024evidence,braspenning2024flamingo}. 
For lower-mass halos, such as those of isolated star-forming galaxies ($M_h \sim 10^{12}$), the circumgalactic medium was primarily studied using quasar absorption spectroscopy~\citep{ tumlinson2011large,prochaska2011probing,werk2016coshalos,chen2024circumgalactic}, using metal lines to study the kinematics, temperature, and baryonic mass of the circumgalactic medium.

In principle, FRBs offer a complementary probe of the circumgalactic gas in $L^*$ halos, by virtue of being insensitive to temperatures and metallicities.
The challenge in doing so is that FRB DMs are ``confusion-limited'': while the signal from $L^*$ halos is observable, it is difficult to separate any particular DM signal of interest along the line of sight from the others.
Several methods have been proposed to circumvent this problem, including angular cross-correlations with galaxy surveys~\citep{2019arXiv190102418M} and pencil-beam spectroscopic surveys~\citep{lee2022constraining,simha2020disentangling,simha2021estimating,khrykin2024flimflam}.
The bottleneck is that these approaches either require many FRB sources or are expensive in telescope time.

In this paper, we isolate \dmhcgm\ by simply discarding sources for which other contributions to $\dmhm$ may dominate.
We define \dmhcgm\ to include all contributions to the DM integral between the spatial extent of the stellar disk and $\sim 7.5$ Mpc outside the galaxy. This definition is motivated by the dimensions of the CAMELS snapshots (15 Mpc). We argue that this definition is better-suited for comparisons between simulated and observed FRB sightlines. It is more comprehensive of circumgalactic electrons than, for instance, a definition which fix a boundary between the CGM and IGM at some multiple of $R_{200c}$. Those definitions which invoke a dependence on halo mass, which is nontrivial to measure. This definition incurs a bias due to the IGM contribution integrated over $15$ Mpc simulated sightline, but this is a small effect on the order of $1-10$ pc cm$^{-3}$ relative to the per-sightline uncertainty ($\sigmacosmic^2 + S_j^2$, defined later).

We introduce and explore the $M^*-\dmh$ relation for isolated star-forming FRB host galaxies at $z < 0.2$, in analogy to ``cluster scaling relations'' which correlate baryonic observables in more massive halos~\citep{battaglia2012on,giodini2013}.
%~\citep{mushotzky1984xray,bryan2008statistical,markevitch1998lxt}.
We define a sub-sample of these $z < 0.2$ hosts, constructed to minimize the bias and scatter in $\dmhcgm$, and use the sub-sample to demonstrate the ability of FRBs to constrain feedback models through the $M^*-\dmh$ relation (\S\ref{sec:sample}).
We show by ray-tracing snapshots in the CAMELS suite of hydro-simulations that the $M^*-\dmh$ relation is a sensitive probe of feedback.
In \S\ref{sec:camels} we compare our sub-sample to theoretical predictions from three sub-grid models implemented in the CAMELS suite of hydrodynamical simulations to constrain models for star formation and galactic winds originating from supernova and AGN feedback in isolated $L^*$ galaxies.

\section{Constructing the observed $M^*-\dmh$ relation}\label{sec:sample}
We aggregate all known FRB host galaxies with the goal of exploring the $M^*-\dmh$ relation for isolated, star forming galaxies.
These include sources from~\citep{macquart2020census,bhandari2020host,lanman2022sudden,leewadell2023host,michilli2023subarcminute,ravi2023deep,gordon2023demographics,bhardwaj2024host,law2024deep,sharma2024preferential,connor2024gas,cassanelli2024fast,shannon2024commensal,chime2025catalog}.
We impose a redshift cutoff of $z < 0.2$ to balance a trade-off: 
on one hand, a cutoff that is more distant adds statistical constraining power by including more sources.
On the other hand, a less distant cutoff adds several advantages.
It makes our measurement of $\dmhcgm$ insensitive to redshift evolution effects and the cosmic contribution, whose mean \avgdmcosmic and scatter \sigmacosmic\ will add extra noise which depends on feedback and increases with increasing redshift~\citep{jaroszynski2019fast,batten2021cosmic, walker2024dispersion}. 
It also reduces the fraction of putative host galaxies which are fainter than the depth of optical surveys~\citep{seebeck2021effects,marnoch2023unseen,jahnsschindler2023how,hewitt2024repeating}.

For each FRB, we calculate $\dmhm$ according to Eq.~\ref{eq:dmh_definition}, using the NE2001~\citep{cordes2002ne2001} model to estimate $\dmmwism$ and the~\citet{yamasaki2020galactic} model to estimate $\dmmwcgm$, and the open-source\footnote{\texttt{average\_DM} function in \texttt{https://github.com/FRBs}} implementation of $\langle \dmcosmic \rangle$.
All quantities in this work are calculated using the \textit{Planck} cosmology for a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe of $H_0 = 67.7~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, $\Omega_m=0.31$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.69$ \citep{collaboration2020planck}. 
Where available, we use published stellar mass measurements derived from spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting. For fits using a Kroupa initial mass function (IMF)~\citep{gordon2023demographics,sharma2024preferential,connor2024gas} we scale stellar masses by 0.03 dex~\citep{madau2014cosmic} to standardize them to a~\citet{chabrier2003galactic} IMF.
For the ten sources that lack $M^*$ measurements from SED fitting,
we fit publicly available photometry from GALEX, DECaLS, PanSTARRS, and WISE using CIGALE~\citep{boquien2019cigale} assuming a Chabrier IMF and a delayed-$\tau$ star formation history. 
These fits converged for eight of the ten targets. 
The remaining two galaxies have stellar mass measurements from the  NASA Extragalactic Database-Local Volume Survey~\citep{cook2023completeness}.
We have validated that the stellar masses derived using this method are consistent with the linear relation between $M^*$ and r-band light ($M_r$) characterized in~\citet{mahajan2018galaxy}. They differ by at most 0.5 dex over the range $10^8 < M^*/M_\odot < 10^{11}$, which is consistent with the scatter on that relation ($\pm 0.45$ dex at 1$\sigma$). Furthermore, once converted from a Salpeter to a Chabrier IMF, the CIGALE and NED-LVS mass estimates are consistent within fitting uncertainties for the two sources for which both data are available.
The $M^*-\dmh$ relation for all FRB sightlines at $z < 0.2$ is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:host_dm} and~\ref{fig:camels}.

\subsection{Defining a de-biased sample}\label{sec:debias}
The difficulty in measuring the CGM component of FRB host galaxies using \dmhm\ (Eq.~\ref{eq:dmh_definition}) comes from unmodeled contributions along the line of sight (Eq.~\ref{eq:dmh_contributions})
In other words, for an FRB chosen at random, $\dmhm$ is not only a noisy estimator of $\dmhcgm$, but it is likely biased as well.
We mitigate these issues by applying cuts to our dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:host_dm}). 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Host galaxies which are embedded in massive galaxy clusters have contributions to the total DM on $\sim$ Mpc scales add a large positive bias to \dmhm which may contribute several hundred pc cm$^{-3}$. Galaxy cluster FRBs include FRB 20220914A and FRB 20220509G~\citep{connor2023deep}, FRBs 20231206A, 20230203A, and 20231204A~\citep{chime2025catalog}. These account for some of the largest $\dmh$ data points in Fig.~\ref{fig:host_dm} and are thus excluded from our analysis. \msbold{For XX of our sightlines covered in DESI and WISE, we cross-match sources against the \citet{wen2018catalogue} catalog to check that their hosts are not within $R_{200c}$ of any cluster within $\Delta z = 0.01$ of the FRB host. For the remaining sightlines, we are unable to assess whether they live in clusters without explicitly using DM-related information, but the absence of large outlier \dmhm\ values in Fig.~\ref{fig:host_dm} and the rarity of FRB hosts in clusters in the literature suggest that hosts in clusters do not dominate the remaining sightlines.} %In addition the rate of intervening halos within their $R_{200c}$ is $< 1$ per sightline at $z < 0.2$ for  $M_h \gtrsim 10^{12} M_\odot$ ~\citep{mcquinn2014locating}. 
    \item The host 
    galaxy ISM can be the dominant contributor to \dmh~\citep{cassanelli2024fast}, making $\dmh$ an overestimate of $\dmhcgm$. We suppress (but cannot completely eliminate) this contribution by eliminating sightlines with host galaxies more inclined than $i = 70^\circ$. These include FRB 20210603A \citep[$i \sim 83^\circ$; ][]{cassanelli2024fast}, FRB 20220207C \citep[$i \sim 74-80^\circ$;][]{law2024deep,bhardwaj2024selection}, FRB 20230203A ($i \sim 77-81^\circ$) and FRB 20231005A ($i \sim 81^\circ$), for which we apply the InclinationZoo estimator~\citep{kourkchi2020cosmicflows} to publicly-available SDSS and Pan-STARRS imaging, verifying that that measurements are robust between both images. FRB 20231120A ($i \approx 81^\circ$)~\citep{sharma2024preferential}, and FRB 20240201A ($i \approx 72^\circ$) and FRB 20240310A ($i \approx 76^\circ$) from~\citet{shannon2024commensal}, whose host galaxy properties will be presented in Gordon et al. (in prep).
    \item We implement a magnitude cut ($M_R < -17$, corresponding to $M^* \sim 10^{9} M_\odot$). This is chosen to exclude known dwarf hosts (e.g. FRB 20121102A) from our sub-sample -- the other two of the three known dwarf hosts~\citep[FRB 20190520B \& 20210117A][]{bhandari2023nonrepeating,hewitt2024repeating} are beyond $z > 0.2$, so they are already cut by our redshift horizon. 
    \item The Milky Way ISM adds scatter due to the 20\% uncertainty on the DM$_\mathrm{NE2001}$ estimate of the DM. We exclude sources at Galactic latitudes below $|b| < 6^\circ$, excluding FRB 20210405I~\citep{driessen2024frb}. 
    \item The circum-source medium of the FRB on potentially $\sim$ parsec scales. This contribution is the least constrained but even for sources considered to be extreme, it is partially explained by H$\alpha$-based estimation of the DM from the circum-source environment~\citep{tendulkar2017host, ocker2022large,caleb2023subarc,bernalescortes2025empirical}. However, it is thought that the presence of a large circum-burst contribution may be indicated by the FRB scattering timescale~\citep{cordes2016radio} and in some cases the rotation measures as well~\citep{michilli2018extreme,mannings2023}. We exclude FRBs whose scattering timescales at 600 MHz exceed 5 ms to reduce the bias from the circumburst medium, excluding FRB 20230222A~\citep{chime2025catalog} from our sample.
    \item So-called ``low-DM'' FRB sightlines. For surveys with large ($\gtrsim 1'$) localization regions, the low total DM can be used to make a host galaxy association, but only by imposing a DM-inferred distance cutoff. However, these associations bias the sample because of the explicit selection bias on DM$_{tot}$ and therefore $\dmhm$. To estimate the host CGM contribution, which is upper bounded by~\dmh, it is essential to exclude low-DM selected sightlines since they are not representative estimates of \dmhm. %There are nine FRB hosts identified this way described in~\citet{bhardwaj2024host,michilli2023subarcminute,Ibik2024host}: They include FRBs 20180814A, 20181030A, 20181220A, 20181223C, 20190418A, 20190110C, 20190425A, 20200223B, as well as 20190303A (note its repeat burst FRB 20231204A). Analyzing this sub-sample separately confirms that these bursts are drawn from the low-\dmhm\ tail of the FRB population.
    \item Similarly, FRBs 20200120E~\citep{bhardwaj2021nearby, kirsten2022repeating} and 20240209A\citep{shah2024repeating}, which have $b \sim 5 R_\mathrm{eff}$, are excluded from our measurement since they may systematically underestimate the average host galaxy CGM (large offset and elliptical, gas-poor host).
\end{enumerate}

Applying these cuts leaves 24 estimates of \dmhm (Table~\ref{tab:sample}).
We estimate the uncertainty of each individual \dmhm\ estimate to be
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{i}^2 = \sigmacosmic(z)^2 + (0.2 \dm_\mathrm{i,NE2001})^2.
    \label{eq:sigma_i_def}
\end{equation}

In our analysis we use values of \sigmacosmic(z) measured from snapshots taken from large-volume hydrodynamic simulations which more accurately model large-scale fluctuations not captured in the small CAMELS volumes.
The choice of \sigmacosmic(z) is important: on one hand it is desirable to define our uncertainties agnostic of feedback; however, \sigmacosmic(z) varies as a function of feedback model, which we are trying to constrain.
For example, IllustrisTNG implements a feedback model stronger than Astrid and weaker than SIMBA, resulting in $\sigma_\textrm{cosmic} \approx 60$ pc cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 0$ and 105 pc cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 0.2$~\citep{walker2024dispersion}, whereas the original Illustris simulation finds $\approx15\%$ lower values for \sigmacosmic due to its more extreme feedback~\citealp[see Fig. 2 of]{jaroszynski2019fast}.
We use the IllustrisTNG values in our fiducial analysis (i.e. feedback-independent uncertainties), but we test the impact of varying \sigmacosmic~ according to the three feedback models being evaluated.

\section{Constructing the Simulated $M^*-\mathrm{DM}_\mathrm{CGM}$ relation}\label{sec:camels}
To connect the observed $M^*-\dmh$ distribution to galaxy formation sub-grid models, we compare the \dmh-stellar mass relation to predictions from the CAMELS suite of hydrodynamical simulations \citep{camels_presentation, camels_data_release,  camels_data_release2}. 
CAMELS is a suite of small-volume (25 Mpc/h)$^3$ hydrodynamical simulations implementing several distinct sub-grid ``prescriptions'' found in the Astrid, IllustrisTNG, and SIMBA simulations. These prescriptions describe the unresolved small-scale physics that regulate galaxy formation and evolution, such as star formation and feedback and AGN activity. 
One major goal of the CAMELS project is to produce theory predictions to independently constrain the suppression of the matter power spectrum induced by baryonic feedback relative to a dark-matter only simulation, as measured in current and next-generation weak lensing surveys such as the Dark Energy Survey~\citep{chen2023constraining,aric_o2023des} and LSST/Rubin. 
Cosmological inference from weak lensing will likely need independent gas probes to simultaneously constrain the suppression factor $S(k,z)$. \citet{medlock2025constraining} recently showed with CAMELS that FRB statistics can indeed predict the degree of suppression of the matter power spectrum due to baryonic feedback. We extend~\citet{medlock2024probing} by measuring the $M^*-\dmh$ relation and comparing it to theory predictions as implemented in SIMBA, IllustrisTNG, and Astrid as implemented with CAMELS.

Using snapshots from each simulation outputted at different epochs of the simulation (here, snapshots at $z = 0.0,~0.1,~$ and 0.2), it is possible to make theory predictions for the $M^*-\dmh$ relation.
We denote the theory prediction as $\dm^\mathcal{M}_j$ as a function of mass bin $j$ and feedback model $\mathcal{M}$, where $\mathcal{M}$ indexes either Astrid, IllustrisTNG, or SIMBA. Since each FRB host $i$ falls into a mass bin $j$, we may equivalently index our model predictions as $\dm^\mathcal{M}_i$.
Since we expect that our measurements (\dmh) slightly overestimate the CGM contribution, \dmh > \dmhcgm\ for the sub-sample described in \S\ref{sec:debias}, 
our model predictions must be constructed to \emph{underestimate} \dmhcgm in order to derive robust constraints.
Doing so allows us to rule out a given feedback model $\mathcal{M}$ if there is no value of \dmhcgm\ which satisfies $\dmh > \dmhcgm > \dm^\mathcal{M}_j$.
We follow the procedure developed in~\citet{medlock2024probing}, slightly modified to guarantee that $\dmhcgm > \dm^\mathcal{M}_j$.

We start with both halo catalogs and simulation snapshots from the \texttt{1P} set of simulations in CAMELS corresponding to the fiducial values from each simulation $(A_{SN1} = A_{SN2} = A_{AGN1} = A_{AGN2} = 1)$. 
We use \texttt{yt}~\citep{turk2011yt} to interpolate the electron density field onto a regular grid. 
The electron density field is then integrated along one of the grid axes to make two-dimensional maps of the DM integrated through the snapshot.
After removing sightlines intercepting more than one halo within its $R_{200c}$, like in criteria 2 in \S\ref{sec:debias},
these sightlines may be indexed by their closest halo in the snapshot, which we denote $k$.
The remaining sightlines are either ``one-halo'' or ``zero-halo'' sightlines. 

The ``zero-halo'' sightlines which do not pass within 0.1 $R_{200c}$ of a halo are used to measure the IGM contribution from a single snapshot, which is found by~\citet{medlock2024probing} to be negligible. 
Owing to the small box size, the contribution from the IGM within a snapshot never exceeds 10 pc cm$^{-3}$.

The simulation sightlines are binned by their normalized impact parameter $b = r_\perp/R_{200}$. 
We select sightlines whose impact parameters lie within some annulus $b_{\rm min} < b < b_{\rm max}$ around a halo.
Many one-halo sightlines pass through the same halo, so we select at random one sightline per distinct halo in the catalog, discarding all the rest.
This corrects for a preferential sampling of massive halos proportional to their 2d-projected area.

We choose $b_{\rm min} = 0.13$ in our fiducial analysis to avoid the \dmhism\ contribution, and choose $b_{\rm max} = 0.2$. 
Our fiducial choices of $b_{\rm min}$ and $b_{\rm max}$ are conservative in the sense that larger values of $b_{\rm min}$ and $b_{\rm max}$ decrease $\widehat{\dm}_\mathcal{M}$. 
However, we will later explore variations in both $b_{\rm min}$ and $b_{\rm max}$.
Our choice of $b_{\rm min}$ is chosen to exclude the possibility that the DM excess in \dmhcgm\ as calculated from our rays is contaminated by the ISM of the host.
Our $\dm^\mathcal{M}_j$ predictions are slightly sensitive to the host offset distribution of FRBs: FRB sightlines highly offset from their gas halos may exclude a portion of \dmhcgm\ from their sightlines~\citealp[see Fig. 5 in]{medlock2024probing}. Though high-offset FRB sightlines are known to exist~\citep{kirsten2022repeating,shah2024repeating,eftekhari2024massive}, it is thought that this does not happen for the majority of FRB sources which are robustly associated with their hosts, whose impact parameter never exceeds 5 $R_\mathrm{eff}$\citep{heintz2020host,shah2024repeating}. Given that the half-light radius $R_\mathrm{eff} \sim 0.015 R_{vir}$\citep{kravtsov2013size}, we may conclude that $b \lesssim 0.1 R_{vir}$ for real FRB sightlines in our sample, even though the projected offset of some sources is not well-constrained by the highly elongated localization ellipse~\citep{chime2025catalog}.

After restricting the simulation sightlines to a range of $b$ values, the values of the DM integrated through the snapshot ($\dmsnap$) are binned by stellar mass and divided by two to account for the fact that observed FRB sightlines skewer halfway through their host halos on average. Then we take a weighted average of $\dmsnap$ over all sightlines $k$ whose associated halos lie in stellar mass bin $j$.
The model predictions are the mean and variance of \dmhcgm. These are referred to as $\widehat{\dm}$ and $S^2$ and are dependent on feedback model $\mathcal{M}$. 

\begin{align} 
    \widehat{\dm} &= \widehat{\dm}_{j,\mathcal{M}} = \sum_{k \in j} w_k \dmsnap^{\mathcal{M}} \\
    S^2 &= \sum_{k \in j} w_k (\dmsnap^{\mathcal{M}} - \widehat{\dm}_{j,\mathcal{M}})^2.
\end{align}


Our fiducial analysis has weights $w_k \propto \mathrm{SFR}$ of the halo associated with simulated halo $h$, which reflect the observed occurrence of FRB sources within star-forming galaxies~\citep{bhandari2020host,loudas2025unveiling}. We also calculate the standard deviation to capture the sightline-to-sightline variance.
We explore alternative weightings with uniform weighting over all simulated halos ($w_k = 1$) and $w_k \propto M^* \times \mathrm{SFR}$.

\section{Model Selection}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:camels} we have plotted the measured $M^*-\dmh$ relation over the fiducial model predictions, which averages FRB sightlines between $0.13 < b < 0.20$ in the $z = 0.1$ snapshot for each sub-grid model with $w_k \propto$ SFR. 
Even though our sample was constructed to satisfy $\dmh > \dmhcgm > \langle \dmsnap \rangle$, we see the opposite behavior in the ASTRID simulations at $M^* \sim 10^{10-11}$, where $\langle \dmsnap \rangle > \dmh$.
Using Bayesian model selection, we show that this discrepancy between ASTRID and our observational data is statistically significant over a wide range of model assumptions.

In this formalism, given that one of these three fiducial models -- ASTRID, IllustrisTNG, and SIMBA -- describes reality, we may compute the Bayes factor for each feedback model to choose between them. In reality, the space of feedback models is continuous and spans a much wider range than the three discrete cases here, but our analysis could be extended in future work to cover the wider range explored e.g. in the CAMELS-LH (Latin Hypercube) dataset.

The Bayes factor $K$ that compares feedback models $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ is the ratio of their posterior probabilities $P(\mathcal{M} | \dm^\mathrm{Macquart}_{\mathrm{h,i}})$:

\begin{equation}
    K = \dfrac{P(\mathcal{A} | \dm^\mathrm{Macquart}_{\mathrm{h,i}})}{P(\mathcal{B} | \dm^\mathrm{Macquart}_{\mathrm{h,i}})}
    \label{eq:kdef}
\end{equation}

In turn, $P(\mathcal{M} | \dm^\mathrm{Macquart}_{\mathrm{h,i}})$ can be calculated from the joint posterior combining all sightlines $i$, marginalized over nuisance parameters. If the data $\dm^\mathrm{Macquart}_\mathrm{h,i}$ (hereafter $\dm^\mathcal{M}_{i}$) were an unbiased estimator of the quantity $\dm^\mathcal{M}_{i}$ predicted by model $\mathcal{M}$ for the $i$th sightline, then a Gaussian likelihood with width $\sigma^2_\mathrm{tot,i} = \sigma_\mathrm{cosmic,i}^2 + S_i^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{CBM}^2$ for uncertainties due to large-scale structure $\sigma_\mathrm{cosmic,i}$, halo-to-halo scatter in the model prediction for that sightline $S_i$, and uncertainty from the circumburst contribution $\sigma_\mathrm{CBM}$ would be appropriate. However, more realistically, $\dm_i$ should be seen as an an upper limit on $\widehat{\dm}_{i}$ because it includes some unknown contribution from the circumburst medium (CBM) and the host galaxy ISM, which we lump together as $\dmhcbmi$. 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{i} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_\mathrm{tot,i}^2}}\exp\left[ -\dfrac{(\dm_{\mathrm{i}} - \dmhcbmi - $\dm^\mathcal{M}_{i}$)^2}{2\sigma_\mathrm{tot,i}^2} \right] \label{eq:logl}
\end{equation}
In this case the posterior probability of model prediction $\dm^\mathcal{M}_{i}$ being allowed given the data $\dm_i$ is given by marginalizing the likelihood $\mathcal{L}_{i}$ over all possible values of $\dmhcbmi > 0$. We use uninformative priors (uniform for each $\dmhcbmi > 0$) and $\pi(\sigma_\mathrm{CBM}) = \sigma_\mathrm{CBM} / \sigma_i^2$ for $\sigma_\mathrm{CBM} > 0$). Starting with Eq.~\ref{eq:logl}, we first marginalize over $\dmhcbmi$ for each sightline:

\begin{align}
P&(\mathcal{M}|\sigma_\mathrm{CBM},\mathrm{each~}\dm_i,) = \int \pi(\dmhcbmi)\mathcal{L}_{i}(\dmhcbmi,\sigma_\mathrm{CBM}) \\
&= \dfrac{1}{2}\left(1 +\mathrm{erf}\left(\dfrac{\dm_i - \dm^\mathcal{M}_{i}}{2\sigma_\mathrm{tot,i}^2} \right)\right)
\intertext{combine the probabilities over sightlines, and then marginalize over $\sigma_\mathrm{CBM}$:}
P&(\mathcal{M}|\mathrm{all~}\dm_i)= \int_0^\infty \pi(\sigma_\mathrm{CBM}) \prod_i P(\mathcal{M}|\dm_i,\sigma_h) ~d\sigma_\mathrm{CBM}.
\label{eq:marginalizedpi}
\end{align}

Each row corresponds to an analysis variant where we test our conclusions robustness to changes in the analysis procedure. If we make the underlying assumption that one of the three fiducial CAMELS models accurately captures the impact of feedback in star forming $L^*$ halos, then our fiducial analysis offers ``strong evidence'' \citep{jeffreys1998theory} against the feedback model implemented in Astrid.
Our fiducial results use the $z = 0.1$ snapshot, with sightlines selected between $0.13 < b < 0.20$, followed by calculating the weighted mean and the scatter of \dmhcgm\ in several mass bins, where the weights for each sightline in a mass bin are proportional to the star formation rate of that halo.
To ensure that our results are robust, we vary the parameters of this fiducial analysis.
To ensure that our results are insensitive to redshift evolution, we repeat the analysis with the same snapshots at different redshifts (``Fiducial $z = 0.0$'' and ``Fiducial $z = 0.2$'' variants).
To verify that we are not picking up the host galaxy ISM in our simulated sightlines, we decrease $b_{\rm min}$ from $0.13 \to 0.10$ (``0.10 < b < 0.20'' variant), finding a slight increase in the significance ($\log_{10} K = -4.4 \to -5.4$).
To verify that our results are insensitive to the FRB-host galaxy offset distribution, we decrease both $b_\mathrm{min}$ and $b_\mathrm{max}$ by 0.3 (``0.10 < b < 0.16'' variant).
We explore different weighting schemes which allow FRB sources to trace either the star formation rate, stellar mass ($w_h \propto M^*$), both ($w_h \propto \mathrm{SFR}\times M^*$), or neither property (an FRB in every halo, $w_h = 1$).

We also investigate the possibility that $\dmcosmic(z)$ for our sightlines may not be well-predicted by its mean value $\langle\dmcosmic(z)\rangle$ averaged over all sightlines out to a given $z$.
We test for this possibility by varying the amount of $\langle\dmcosmic\rangle$ subtracted in Eq.~\ref{eq:dmh_definition} by $50\%$ in either direction for every sightline in our sample.
Our insensitivity to this large shift in the cosmic DM can be explained by the low redshift of our sample (thus, small \avgdmcosmic values).
The ``Low-end only'' and ``High-end only'' analysis variants split our sample in two based on the stellar mass of the host ($10^{9.8} M_\odot$). They illustrate that the low end of the $M^*-\dmh$ relation is not very constraining, with the caveat that the limited mass resolution of CAMELS ($1.3\times 10^7 h^{-1} M_\odot$) may more strongly influence the low-mass end of our theory predictions. Instead, most of the discriminating power comes from the high-mass end of the $M^*-\dmh$ relation, as seen visually in Fig.~\ref{fig:camels}.
We check that our results are also insensitive to the results of our SED fitting by decreasing all the stellar masses by $0.3$ dex (``All $M^*-0.3$ dex'' variant).

It is also robust to the threshold adopted for host associations as computed by the PATH algorithm~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic}, by tightening the association criteria from the industry standard ($P(O|x) > 0.90$) to $P(O|x) > 0.95$, where $P(O|x)$ is the posterior probability that the FRB is associated with its putative host.
Finally, we explore the effect of changing \sigmacosmic on our results. Ideally, \sigmacosmic could be measured from a large simulation volume employing the same sub-grid model in which the model predictions were generated. However, the small-volume CAMELS run of Astrid has a different mass resolution from the large-volume production run of Astrid and puts a direct calculation of $\sigmacosmic$ out of the scope of this work. We check that doubling \sigmacosmic with respect to the fiducial analysis reduces our statistical significance from $\log_{10}K = -5.4 \to -3.8$, but does not affect the qualitative interpretation of our results. In addition, we explore the effect of a feedback-dependent \sigmacosmic\ in the ``Varying \sigmacosmic'' analysis. 
Given that changing the feedback prescription from IllustrisTNG (weaker feedback) to Illustris (stronger feedback) decreases \sigmacosmic\ by $\sim 15\%$, we generously scale $\sigmacosmic \to 1.5\sigmacosmic$ to calculate the posterior in the weak feedback scenario (Astrid) and $\sigmacosmic \to 0.8\sigmacosmic$ for the strong feedback scenario (Astrid). We find that our conclusions remain affected, and that the statistical significance of our conclusions is increased. 

\section{Discussion}
Of the three fiducial simulations considered, our data strongly disfavor Astrid, which is considered to have the ``weakest'' feedback, when compared against either IllustrisTNG or SIMBA. Several analysis variants demonstrate that this conclusion is insensitive to a range of astrophysical assumptions.

% Is it AGN or stellar feedback?
It is hard to say using our sample whether stellar or AGN feedback is principally responsible for the divergence at $M^* \gtrsim 10^{10} M_\odot$. This is because our sample consists only of the star-forming host galaxies of FRBs, which typically show a low degree of AGN activity. In addition, hydrodynamical simulations of stellar and AGN feedback in isolated galaxies~\citep{newton2013study} suggest that stellar feedback dominates in isolated galaxies in the absence of galaxy merger activity. This leads to the notion that \dmh\ at high stellar masses is primarily sensitive to stellar feedback. 

On the other hand, as pointed out in~\citet{ni2023camels}, the principal difference which sets apart Astrid from IllustrisTNG and SIMBA is the kinetic AGN feedback in ASTRID, which is activated in a more stringent way and with a lower maximum efficiency than in the IllustrisTNG model. In addition, it is known that prescriptions for black hole seeding and dynamics differ and are potentially less realistic in the lower resolution Astrid runs in CAMELS (referred to in~\citep{ni2023camels} as CAMELS-Astrid) than in the production run of Astrid. SIMBA, which predicts the lowest \dmhcgm, notably features anisotropic AGN feedback~\citep{anglesalcazar2017black,dave2019simba} in which collimated jets expel gas from their halos, and it is thought that anisotropic feedback more effectively expels gas to larger radii than isotropic feedback. These reasons suggest that AGN activity may play an important role in shaping the $M^*-\dmh$ relation.

It is possible that both stellar \emph{and} AGN feedback play a role in this mass range, since the reservoir of ionized gas in the CGM couples to both stellar and AGN feedback processes. This causes a complex interplay between star formation and AGN activity. For instance, at low black hole masses and high redshift, stellar feedback regulates the growth of supermassive black holes by limiting their accretion rate~\citep{anglesalcazar2017black}. In addition, ~\citet{booth2013interaction} suggests~\citealp[contrary to]{newton2013study} that stellar and AGN feedback have opposing influences on the star formation rate: it is plausible that similar competing effects may shape the $M^*-\dmh$ relation. 

As pointed out by~\citet{medlock2025constraining} our results can be compared with other measurements of baryonic feedback from weak lensing analyses via the matter power spectrum suppression factor. In simulations like CAMELS, this is defined as $S(k) \equiv P_{\rm hydro}/P_{\rm nbody}$ calculated from the $z \sim 0$ snapshots used for our $M^*-\dmh$ measurements. The power spectra are referenced to those extracted from a corresponding gravity-only simulation which does not model baryonic feedback. The suppression factor measured in CAMELS snapshots are plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:sk} for the three models considered here. 

In cosmic shear observations, the suppression factor is typically measured using the observed cosmic shear power spectra referenced to gravity-only predictions of the nonlinear matter power spectrum. The suppression factor measured in Dark Energy Survey~\citep{chen2023constraining} and in Subaru Hyper Suprime Cam~\citep{terasawa2025exploring} are extrapolated in redshift to $z \sim 0$ for comparison with simulations. 

At face value, our analysis disfavoring Astrid poses a challenge to models of weak feedback and favor the stronger feedback seen in IllustrisTNG and SIMBA.
However, our comparison is subject to two caveats. 
First, our measurements have only constrained the volume-filling ionized component of the CGM of FRB hosts.
If the CGM is instead clumpy then it is possible that only a fraction of sightlines $f$ encounter the CGM -- in that case, a larger number of FRB sightlines (increased by $\sim 1/f$) would be needed to place meaningful constraints on $\dmhcgm$.
Second, our statistical comparison assumes that one of the three fiducial variants of the CAMELS simulations describes reality. 
This is almost certainly not the case, since feedback spans a wide and continuous range of model parameters even within the broader CAMELS simulations.
Finally, our measurement does not fully account for the impact of feedback since the small volume of CAMELS snapshots do not sufficiently sample the high-mass end of the halo mass function, which is thought to contribute significantly to baryonic feedback as measured in cosmic shear. For example, while our data directly probe feedback from $M_h \sim 10^{12} M_\odot$ halos at $z \sim 0$, the peak of the redshift distribution of galaxies used in the cosmic shear analysis of DES is at $z \sim 0.5$ ($z \sim 0.9$ for Subaru HSC) and targets halo masses of $M_h \sim 10^{14}$. In this sense, our results may be considered as a lower limit on the impact of feedback: an upper limit, not an unbiased measurement, of $S(k,z=0)$. 

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
In this paper, we have explored the $M^*-\dmh$ relation for the first time, showing that it diverges as a function of feedback model in isolated galaxies $M^* \sim M^*_\mathrm{MW}~(M_h \sim 10^{12})$. Our findings are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We have used simulations and a sample of FRB data, introducing the $M^*-\dmh$ relation for the first time.
    \item We find -- contrary to weak feedback models -- that \dmhm tends to be \emph{anti-}correlated with stellar mass in star-forming, $L^*$ galaxies.
    \item Our measurements constrain the gas environments of isolated star-forming galaxies at $z = 0$ and $M_h \sim 10^{12}$, allowing us to place the first lower limit on the strength of feedback via the suppression factor of the matter power spectrum $S(k,z=0)$: a measurement which is complementary to cosmic shear analyses.
\end{enumerate}

This analysis could readily be extended in several ways. As mentioned previously, future work should marginalize over a realistic continuum of feedback scenarios. Going to higher redshifts better samples the halo mass function using FRB hosts living in groups and clusters like those presented in~\cite{connor2023deep,chime2025catalog}. Higher redshifts can also be used to study the redshift evolution of the gas environments of galaxies over cosmic time. However, the foreground posed by the IGM will demand a larger number of sources, since $\sigmacosmic$ increases with increasing redshift. In the opposite direction, widefield~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian} and all-sky FRB telescopes~\citep{lin2022burstt} will soon deliver an order-of-magnitude increase in FRBs closer than $<$ 400 Mpc. Doing so will extend the $M^*-\dmh$ relation downwards in stellar and halo mass, which can allow baryonic feedback to be explored in dark-matter dominated systems like dwarf galaxies. In the long run this could open up the field of ``near-field FRB cosmology''.
