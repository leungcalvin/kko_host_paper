\begin{abstract}
Out of 269 bright (S/N $> 20$) fast radio bursts detected by the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) between December 2022 and February 2024, we present 83 fast radio bursts localized to 2'' by $\sim 1'$ precision. Of these 83 bursts, we use the PATH algorithm to associate them to 18 new host galaxies, and present spectroscopic redshifts for 14. Previous work has focused on using host galaxies of FRBs to understand the progenitors of FRBs; in this paper we assess the ability of FRBs to understand the gas in their hosts in a low-redshift ($z \sim 0.1$) dataset where contamination from the intergalactic medium (IGM) is minimized. 
The host galaxies of these FRBs show remarkable diversity. We use repeat bursts from a previously-reported source to constrain the gas properties of a starbursting pair of interacting galaxies. Another FRB is associated with a luminous galaxy falling into the rich cluster Abell 576. We demonstrate the use of low-redshift FRBs to probe the gas dynamics of their host galaxies in the context of their larger-scale environments. In the case of FRB20231204A we use the dispersion measure excess of the FRB to argue that the starburst generated by the merger is dominated by neutral gas. In the case of FRB20231206A, we use the large dispersion measure excess clarify the velocity structure of the cluster and to constrain the cluster gas fraction far beyond the reach of X-ray measurements. Finally, we use the full sample to make a direct Local Universe measurement of DM$_\textrm{host}$ and $\sigma_\textrm{host}$, two critical parameters in the use of FRBs as cosmic probes. In addition a cross-match of our catalog with wide-area radio catalogs (VLASS, LoTSS, and FIRST) reveals two persistent radio source (PRS) candidates associated with the two repeating sources in our sample.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are short-duration radio pulses whose shocking diversity has earned them a reputation as perhaps the most mysterious class of astrophysical transients~\citep{cordes2019fast,petroff2022fast}. While thousands of FRBs have been discovered~\citep{collaboration2021chime} spanning several orders of magnitude in luminosity, rise time, burst duration, and activity timescale, their origins remain unknown and their potential as cosmological probes remains untapped -- bottlenecked by a lack of robust burst localization and host galaxy identification. 

The host galaxies of FRBs are central to the study of FRB progenitors and their eventual utility as cosmological probes. Following in the footsteps of the gamma-ray burst (GRB) community~\citep{bloom2002observed,fong2022comprehensive},
several early studies have been conducted on FRB host galaxies. With a handful of bursts (5), early studies of sources discovered by the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder~\citep{macquart2020census} found an offset distribution compatible with short GRB hosts and supernovae~\citep{heintz2020host,bhandari2020host}. This small sample was expanded by ~\citet{bhandari2022characterizing} and~\citet{bhardwaj2024host} to 15 and 18 hosts, respectively. Most recently~\citet{law2024deep} and~\citet{sharma2023deep} presented evidence for a broad diversity of delay times and formation channels using a sample of 11 brand-new FRB host galaxies discovered and localized by the Deep Synoptic Array~\citep{kocz2019dsa}. 

These initial studies have all been limited due to the small sample sizes involved. However, we are gradually entering an era of of precise FRB localizations and plentiful host galaxy associations: the Deep Synoptic Array has detected 11 FRBs and localized them to their host galaxies~\citep{law2024deep}, with redshifts ranging from  $z = 0.04$ to $0.62$.
The recent completion of the ASKAP Fast Transient incoherent-sum survey~\citep{shannon2024commensal} has added an optically-complete sample of 43 FRBs and 30 redshifts with redshifts ranging from $z = 0.023$ to $1.02$, many of which have been characterized in the sample of~\citet{gordon2023demographics} (see also~\citet{gordon2023fast}).

This paper expands on these efforts using the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME/FRB;~\citet{collaboration2018chime}) and KKO~\citep{lanman2024chime}, the first of its three VLBI outrigger stations. Using triggered VLBI, we localize 83 FRB sources with an accuracy of $\sim 1' \times 2''$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:localization}), serving as a proof-of-concept of the triggered VLBI technique. The abundance of VLBA and RFC astrometric calibrators at 600 MHz which we observe is encouraging for next-generation FRB telescopes which will rely on similar techniques to pinpoint FRBs: these include CHIME's successor CHORD~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian}, as well as other compact arrays being built as HIRAX~\citep{newburgh2016hirax}, BURSTT~\citep{lin2022burstt}, and CASPA~\citep{luo2024fast}. We systematically investigate the impact of calibrator choice on astrometric accuracy using a sample of single-pulse localizations from pulsars, finding that on this short baseline, our astrometric accuracy is unaffected by our calibrator choice (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

Despite the moderate accuracy of the single-pulse localizations, 18 of the 83 bursts can confidently be associated with bright, nearby host galaxies, as quantified by a conservative application of the Bayesian host association algorithm PATH~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic} (Sec~\ref{sec:association}) and a detailed simulation of the rate of false-positive associations~\citep{andersen2024inprep}.

Finally Sec.~\ref{sec:sample} we describe the 18 hosts in the sample, and highlight a few interesting cases therein. They have brightnesses of $16.4 < m_r < 22.7$ and are all at low redshifts ($z < 0.33$), and like the sample presented in~\citet{law2024deep}, are uniformly selected from the Pan-STARRS~\citep{chambers2016pan, magnier2020pan,waters2020pan, magnier2020pan2, magnier2020pan3, flewelling2020pan} and Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS)~\citep{dey2019overview} imaging surveys. These bright galaxies represent ideal, nearby targets for spatially-resolved followup studies (e.g.~\citep{mannings2021high,woodland2023environments}), where followup facilities can discover faint optical hosts such as dwarf galaxies, which have been known to host a handful of unusual FRBs~\citep{tendulkar2017host,niu2021repeating}. Low-redshift host galaxies are also crucial components of FRB cosmology, providing the tightest constraints on host-galaxy contributions to the total DM along the sightline of an FRB~\citep{james2021z,jahnsschindler2023how}. 

We perform spectroscopic followup using a combination of the 3-meter Shane Reflector at Lick Observatory, our Long and Large Program at Gemini North (PI: Eftekhari), and observations with the Deep Imaging Multi-Object Spectrograph (DEIMOS) mounted on the Keck II telescope at Mauna Kea, Hawaii. In this paper we present spectroscopic redshifts for 14 of the 18 hosts towards low-extinction sightlines ($E(B-V) < 0.3$) through the Milky Way, and reserve detailed spectral characterization for future work. All quantities in this work are calculated using the \textit{Planck} cosmology for a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe of  $H_0 = 67.7~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, $\Omega_M=0.31$ and $\Omega_\mathrm{vac} = 0.69$ \citep{2020}. 

\section{FRB Detection and Initial Localization}\label{sec:localization}
\subsection{Initial burst detection at CHIME}
Between December 9, 2023 and Feb 10, 2024, CHIME/FRB detected $269$ bright (S/N $> 20$) FRB candidates which were later confirmed by two human tsars inspecting their dynamic spectra. 
Following the detection and real-time localization, triggers were sent to dump the full field of view at CHIME and KKO  for $\sim 100$ milliseconds. During this time, baseband data were successfully dumped for the majority of these 269 candidates at CHIME. This enabled the interferometric localization using the CHIME core (``baseband localization'') on arcminute scales. In many but not all of these cases, data were also successfully captured at the KKO outrigger, which was in a commissioning phase until about June 2023. We use the baseband dumps at CHIME and KKO to localize these bursts to 2-arcsecond scale precision along a roughly east-west axis defined by the orientation of the baseline. The localization ellipses are thus highly elongated along a roughly east-west axis, whose orientation angle depends on the exact declination at which each burst was detected.

\subsection{Major-axis localization using CHIME core}
We first localize each burst using the CHIME/FRB baseband localization pipeline, which constrains the north-south extent of the localization ellipse. Two effects limit the astrometric accuracy of the baseband pipeline at the arcminute level: thermal expansion of the focal line, as well as clock offsets between the eastern and western cylinders comprising the CHIME core. These two effects manifest as gradients in the astrometric offset between the localized and true position of known pulsars. They are compensated using auxiliary clock measurements and measurements of the feed temperatures taken from archived environmental monitoring data at the time of the burst arrival. After applying these dynamic correction, a static astrometric offset is applied~\citep{michilli2020analysis}. This correction centers baseband localizations on the true positions of pulsars. 

To validate the quoted accuracy level of this procedure, we apply it to a sample of about 100 pulsars, finding that the RMS accuracy of the pulsar localizations is consistent with the quoted uncertainties (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}). Follow-up observations and EVN localizations of a handful of CHIME-discovered repeating FRBs including FRB20180916A~\citep{collaboration2020periodic,marcote2020repeating}; FRB20200120E~\citep{bhardwaj2021local,kirsten2022repeating}, FRB20201124A~\citep{lanman2022sudden,nimmo2022milliarcsecond}, and FRB20220912~\citep{mckinven2022nine,hewitt2024milliarcsecond} have further confirmed the localization accuracy.

\subsection{Minor-axis localization using VLBI}
Along the east-west axis we add the KKO station. Due to sparse $uv$ coverage and the transience of the signal, it is critical to realistically assess the magnitude of typical localization uncertainties. To establish an astrometric accuracy, we trigger single-pulse voltage dumps on pulsars at a variety of declinations and hour angles (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}). 

The VLBI observations follow the strategy developed in~\citet{leung2021synoptic}: we use full-array baseband dumps to search for bright ($\sim$ 1 Jansky) VLBI calibrators from the Radio Fundamental Catalog (RFC) and VLBA calibrator catalog (see e.g.~\citep{ma1998international,petrov2021wide} and references therein) within the effective field of the baseband dump ($\sim 200$ deg$^2$). At each station, we formed station beams towards each of the $\sim 200$ VLBI calibrators within the field of view. Following~\citep{andrew2024vlbi}, we placed a single correlator pointing in each station beam and correlated each pointing using the~\texttt{PyFX} correlator~\citep{leung2024vlbi}. Visibilities were saved if the calibrator was detected. We detected up to 15 VLBI calibrators per baseband dump this way. Then, we inspected the uncalibrated delay toward each calibrator. In CHIME-KKO VLBI observations the delays measured towards different calibrators typically agree to within 2 ns of each other, indicating that we do not need direction-dependent calibration at this level of delay accuracy. Calibrators with wildly discrepant delays that do not satisfy this criteria can usually be associated with bright sources whose true location is far from the pointing center~\citep{andrew2024vlbi}. We catch these edge cases during delay calibration by disqualifying calibrators whose delays are not within 2 nanoseconds of the median delay computed over other calibrators in the baseband dump. 

After rejecting these misidentified calibrators, we adopt a uniform calibrator selection strategy for our entire sample. We choose the calibrator that minimizes the target-calibrator separation to minimize the impact of the ionosphere, whose impact has already been shown to be minimal on this 66-kilometer baseline~\citep{lanman2024chime}. However, we find using a sample of pulsar localizations that our astrometric accuracy is insensitive to this choice, and that two other reasonable calibrator selection strategies yield a similar astrometric accuracy (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

After a calibrator is selected, we apply delay and phase-calibration to each FRB in the sample to produce calibrated visibilities. For the majority of bursts in the sample, we were able to robustly measure delays in both polarizations. In the majority of cases the delays were coincident between the XX and YY polarization combinations to within 2 nanoseconds. In this scenario we average the delays between the XX and YY polarizations. In other scenarios the delays disagreed by more than 2 nanoseconds. In every scenario where this occurred, the burst was much more strongly detected in one polarization than the other, where the detection was marginal. We attribute this to the intrinsic polarization angle of the burst and the instrumental sensitivity, particularly at the KKO station. In this scenario we use the delay measured in the brighter of the two polarization hands only (XX or YY). The statistical uncertainty on each delay measurement was much smaller than the systematic uncertainties.

Finally, we use a maximum-likelihood delay-mapping procedure to translate these residual time delays into localization contours (See Eq. 4 in~\citet{marcote2020repeating}; also Eq. A1 in~\citet{leung2024vlbi}). Each VLBI localization contour is combined with the baseband localization ellipse as a prior. The combined contour is convolved with a kernel reflecting a conservative estimate of the localization accuracy. Finally, we calculate ellipse parameters (major/minor axes, orientation angle, and central coordinates) from the posterior probability grid.

To characterize the localization accuracy, we apply this procedure to a sample of $\approx 100$ pulsars. In doing so we find that CHIME-KKO observations achieve an astrometric accuracy of $< 1''$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}), except at low declinations, where additional RFI at the KKO site increases the overall RMS error. We conservatively prescribe the minor-axis astrometric uncertainty of the whole sample to 2'' at the $1\sigma$ level estimate the astrometric accuracy of the localizations, and find later on (see Sec.~\ref{sec:sample}) that our sample of robust host galaxy associations is unaffected by this choice.

\section{Host Association}\label{sec:association}
After localizing each burst with VLBI, we use the default priors in the PATH toolkit to assign posterior probabilities to each catalogued galaxy allowed by the combined localization region from baseband and CHIME-KKO VLBI. Each localization region is cross-matched with a widefield galaxy survey. Where possible \textbf{(55/78 fields)}, we cross-match against the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS) to find a host galaxy due to its greater depth. In the remainder of observations \textbf{(23/78 fields)}, we default to Pan-STARRS PS1, which covers the entire CHIME/FRB discovery footprint. \textbf{Note: Waiting for last 5 fields from incomplete PATH run; update these numbers when those are done.}

\textbf{Note: would like somebody to review this} Stars are identified in each field on the basis of their classification in Gaia DR3. After this automated removal, each field is manually inspected for contamination by misclassified stars \textbf{and other failure modes to which I am blissfully unaware thanks to our follow-up team}. Finally, each galaxy candidate in the field is assigned a probability $P(O|x)$ of being the true host of the FRB using the PATH (Probabilistically Associating Transients to their Hosts) framework~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic}.

Accurately assigning these posterior probabilities is difficult when combining shallow, widefield archival imaging with a highly sensitive FRB detector such as CHIME. In this regime, a significant fraction of bursts originates from beyond the maximum redshift out to which a faint FRB host would be detectable. As a point of reference, the faint dwarf host of FRB 20121102A~\citet{tendulkar2017host} would be detectable in the DECaLS $r$-band out to $z \sim 0.2$, depending on the Galactic extinction along the line of sight, implying an extragalactic DM of $\sim 200$. The redshift implied by the median DM of our sample significantly exceeds this (Fig.~\ref{fig:hg_vs_dm}).

The PATH framework accounts for the survey depth mismatch by specifying the ``unseen'' probability $P(U)$, which uses the FRB redshift distribution, the galaxy luminosity function, the localization ellipse footprint, and the optical survey depth to calculate the prior probability that the host galaxy of an individual FRB in a survey is too faint or too confused to be associated with a galaxy with high confidence. We choose values of $P(U)$ for the specific combination of FRBs detected by CHIME on the basis of detailed injection test where mock FRBs are seeded in host galaxies seen in a\textbf{deep image of the COSMOS field...(Bridget?)} and subsequently ``re-detected'' in the shallower optical surveys which we use as the basis for our FRB host associations. This so-called ``sandbox'' us to characterize the fraction
of Type-I errors (i.e. falsely claiming a galaxy association where there is none)~\citep{andersen2024inprep}. Finally, a value of $P(U)$ and a threshold value of $P(O|x)$ are chosen which minimizes the false positive fraction of associations in the sandbox. 

These simulations indicate that the optimal values of $P(U)$ are $0.32$ for the PanSTARRS fields, and $P(U) = 0.15$ for the DECaLS fields. We note that these values are substantially higher than those used in the literature. $P(U) = 0.1$ is used for Pan-STARRS for the DSA-110 host galaxy sample~\citep{law2024deep} and $P(U) = 0.0$ are used in ASKAP-ICS follow-up fields~\citep{shannon2024commensal}. We defer a more extensive justification of these choices to~\citet{andersen2024inprep}, but note that this choice is likely conservative because the sample of bursts detectable by the outrigger stations presented here represent the brightest bursts detected by CHIME, and hence preferentially sample the nearby end of the CHIME redshift distribution~\citep{shin2023inferring}.

\section{Multi-wavelength counterpart cross-matching}
The improved localizations for our sample of bright FRBs is a natural dataset in which to search for multi-wavelength counterparts to FRBs, due to the improved localization, large size (83), and low redshifts of the bursts in the sample. Motivated by the discovery of compact radio counterparts\citealt{chatterjee2017direct,niu2021repeating} and the association of some FRBs with magnetars (\textbf{Can Alice provide some text here...}), we cross-match the localization ellipses with the locations of known GRBs, and search for compact persistent radio sources for the bursts within our sample. 

\subsection{High-energy counterpart search}
We search for temporal and spatial cross-matches between our 83 FRBs and 2589 long and short gamma-ray burst (GRB)  triggers from the GRBWeb database\footnote{\url{https://user-web.icecube.wisc.edu/~grbweb_public/};
Access date October 10, 2023} \citep{GRBweb} and the General Coordinates Network\footnote{\url{gcn.nasa.gov}} between 1991 April 21 and 9 September 2024. Using only the GRBs with 1$\sigma$ localizations $<1\degree$, we do not find any joint spatio-temporal (within 1 week in time and within 3$\sigma$ in spaces) coincidences. We do find three solely spatial cross-matches between FRBs 20231019A, 20230802A, 20230703A and three GRBs: 970306A, 120323A and 920221A*, respectively. Following the method presented in \citep{Curtin2024}, we simulate 1000 sets of GRBs and cross-match each with the FRB sample to estimate the chance coincidence probability of three cross-matches. Each mock sample of GRBs has at least three cross matches, implying a high chance coincidence probability, which is consistent with the large uncertainty regions for these GRBs ($\gtrsim0.5\degree$).  

\subsection{Persistent Radio cross-matching methodology}
Thus far, two FRBs have been confidently associated with persistent radio sources. A third PRS was found to be associated with the active repeating FRB\,20201124A \citep{Bruni2024Nature}, but the larger spatial offset and physical size of the radio source make this source different from the radio counterparts to FRB 20121102A and 20190520B, which have been shown to be compact on milliarcsecond scales~\citep{marcote2017repeating,bhandariprs190520}.
We perform a systematic search for unresolved radio sources coincident with the CHIME-KKO FRBs in the Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty cm (FIRST; \citealt{FIRST}) and the NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS; \citealt{NVSS}) at 1.4\,GHz, as well as the Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS; \citealt{VLASS}) at 3\,GHz. For sources detected in either VLASS or FIRST, we also search the positions in the LOFAR Two-metre Sky Survey (LoTSS) Data Release 2 (DR2; \citealt{LoTSS}) at 144\,MHz. In each survey we searched for radio point sources above its $5\sigma$ detection threshold, using the Canadian Initiative for Radio Astronomy Data Analysis (CIRADA)\footnote{https://cirada.ca/} portal to access the image cutouts. 

In the FIRST and NVSS cutouts we estimated the noise by computing the RMS (i.e. $\sigma$) over the entire 2'' $\times$ 2'' cutout. \section{Host Galaxy Sample}\label{sec:sample}
In this section we highlight some of the interesting sources in our sample. Both repeating FRBs in our sample have persistent radio source (PRS) candidates, but only one has a low chance coincidence probability. Three of our host galaxies are embedded in larger-scale structures: one repeating source in found in a starbursting galaxy merger, one in a massive X-ray cluster, and another in a rich optical cluster, and we comment on the rich potential of FRBs to probe these systems.

\subsection{FRB20231204A: a repeating FRB probing the starburst gas in a galaxy merger}
FRB20231204A is a burst from a repeating source which also produced FRB20190303A, which~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} previously localized to an interacting galaxy pair. In that work, the localization was too large to identify the member galaxy. In this work, the updated localization provided by FRB20231204A indicates that the FRB source likely originates in the member galaxy to the southwest. Briefly, long-slit spectroscopy and Baldwin-Phillips-Terlevich (BPT) diagram classification revealed both galaxies in the interacting system to be star-forming, $\sim L^*$ galaxies; We refer the interested reader to~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} for further details of its spectroscopic characterization.

As pointed out in~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} it is possible to use this system to constrain the gas dynamics in a closely-interacting galaxy system, whose projected separation is $\sim$ 20 kiloparsecs. With a more precise localization it will be possible to do so in great detail; however the current localization accuracy restricts our analysis to an order-of-magnitude estimate. It also requires us to assume that the starburst gas does not ``move'' between the formation of its progenitor and the emission of the burst. Assuming that the delay time between the starburst and the emission of the FRB is much smaller than the dynamical timescale of the galaxy merger ($\sim  10^8$ year), the electron column traversed by the FRB can be used to probe the gas in the starburst. 

Hydrodynamical simulations have suggested that starbursts are fueled by inflows of metal-poor gas from the CGM when the gas loses angular momentum during a galaxy encounter. However, when galaxies in a two-body encounter pass significantly closer than their virial radii as in the case of FRB20231204A, the ISM of the constituent galaxy disks gets disturbed, and in these scenarios it has been proposed that gas in the ISM (rather than the CGM) triggers star formation~\citep{sparre2023gas}. 
This picture is consistent with a rough estimate of the ionized gas content of the host galaxy ISM.~\citet{tempel2014flux} has estimated the luminosity of the merging galaxies to be $2 \times 10^{11} L_\odot$: ten times greater than that of the Milky Way, whose HI surface density exceeds $\Sigma \gtrsim 10^{21}$ cm$^{-3}$ out to $\sim 20$ kiloparsecs~\citep{kalberla2009hi}. We use this as a lower limit on the HI surface density of the gas involved in the starburst, and combine it with the DM budget of the sightline of FRB20231204A, to constrain the ionization state $f_\mathrm{ion}$ of the star-forming gas. The rest-frame DM excess of 112 pc cm$^-3$ constrains the column-averaged ionization fraction to be $\lesssim 34\%$: consistent with a gas column from an ISM, where the ionization fraction is expected to be $\sim 10\%$ (e.g.~\citet{he2013correlation}):
$$\left(\dfrac{f_{ion}}{0.34}\right)\left(\dfrac{\Sigma}{10^{21}\textrm{ cm}^{-3}}\right) \lesssim 112\textrm{ pc cm}^{-3}.$$

FRB20231204A is reminiscent of some other FRB hosts in the literature which show evidence of dynamical  disturbances~\citep{michalowski2021asymmetric,kaur2022fast}. However, those bursts do not have atypical values of DM$_\textrm{host}$ and the merger activity in those scenarios is not as dramatic as in the case of FRB20231204A. In the future, a sample of precisely-localized fast radio bursts as well as auxiliary observations characterizing the neutral gas of the star formation site (e.g.,~\citep{leewadell2023host,yamanaka2024alma,glowacki2023wallaby} may be able to discriminate between the ISM and CGM contributions to star formation during galaxy mergers, as well as the delay-time distribution of FRBs.

\subsection{FRB20231206A: an FRB behind Abell 576 with an associated persistent radio source candidate}\label{subsec:FRB20231206A}
The luminous host galaxy and large dispersion measure excess of FRB20231206A (295 pc cm$^{-3}$ led us to associate its host galaxy to Abell 576~\citep{abell}. This X-ray cluster at $z \approx 0.03$ occupies $\sim 9$ square degrees on the sky and hosts over a thousand galaxies. A subset of the galaxies comprising the cluster comprise a high-velocity tail~\citep{rines2001infall} which has led to its identification as a merger of two subcomponents with a relative velocity of $\sim 9000$ km/s~\citep{dupke2007merger}. The host galaxy of FRB20231206A | one of the most luminous in our sample | is part of the infalling sub-cluster. It has been proposed on the basis of the X-ray morphology of the cluster core that the sub-structure has already made its first passage through the core~\citep{kempner2004chandra} but neither the morphological nor the redshift information can reveal whether the sub-structure is in front or behind the cluster as viewed from earth: two possibilities which are equally likely.

The DM excess of FRB 20231206A can break this degeneracy. At the distance of the cluster center, the host galaxy of FRB 20231206A is transversely displaced by $\approx 5$ Mpc from the cluster center, whose extent is quantified by its $r_{200} = 2.02$ Mpc. At the virial radius the mass of the cluster is between $1-1.4 \times 10^{15} M_\odot$ (~\cite{rines2000infall,girardi1998optical}). We argue that the large (390 pc cm$^{-3}$) dispersion measure excess is more consistent with a geometry where the FRB host lies behind the X-ray cluster. In a scenario where the intracluster medium is completely ionized and where the FRB host lies behind the cluster's dark matter halo, the observed dispersion measure excess then constrains the ionized gas column of the ICM at large impact parameters (in this case, a gas density measurement at 2 $R_{200}$) far beyond the typical reach of X-ray based gas fraction measurements which typically the cluster gas fraction at $\sim R_{2500}-R_{500}$~\citep{mantz2022cosmological} to infer $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$ (see e.g.~\citet{ettori1999rosat}). 
%As in the earlier case, the poor FRB localization limits us to an illustrative order-of-magnitude estiamte. The mass density at a projected radius of 5 Mpc within the aperture implies a mass surface density of $\sigma(<5\text{~Mpc}) \sim 1.5\times10^{13} M_\odot$ Mpc$^{-2}$~\citep{rines2000infall}. Under the assumption of a cosmological ionization fraction, we estimate $f_{gas} \sim 0.6$ at $\sim 2 R_{vir}$. 

Our radio cross-match also revealed a faint persistent radio counterpart in FIRST whose detection significance was $3.9\sigma$ above the RMS flux, but with a large P$_{cc} \sim 0.1$. Its implied luminosity at the distance of the host galaxy is $L_\mathrm{1.4~\mathrm{GHz}} \sim 7\times10^{28}~\mathrm{erg~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Hz}^{-1}$. Similar to FRB\,20231128A, this source is also coincident with the most probable host galaxy (P(O\textbar$x$) = 0.9897) within the $1\sigma$ localization ellipse. Upon revisiting the corresponding VLASS Quick Look (VLASS-QL) query, we identified a source coincident with the FIRST source with a detection significance of $\sim3.5\sigma$ and luminosity of $L_{3.0~\mathrm{GHz}} = 5\times10^{28}~\mathrm{erg~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Hz}^{-1}$. We calculated a $P_\mathrm{cc}$ with FRB\,20231206A of $P_\mathrm{cc} \sim 0.1$, and found no associated LoTSS data at the source position.\par

\section{A direct measurement of the host contribution to the DM budget}
Tracing the origin of DM outliers has been a major question since (and leading up to) the inception of the FRB field. DM budgets have been observed to undershoot (FRB20220319B; see~\citet{ravi2023deep}) and overshoot the total due to an insufficiently-modeled sightline (e.g. FRB 20190520A~\citet{niu2021repeating,lee2023frb}). With every new FRB, observers are motivated to dissect in great detail~\citep{2024arXiv240200505K} the origin of the DM excess after subtracting DM$_\text{IGM}$: is the remainder dominated by a dense circum-source medium, or could it possibly originate from gas along the line of sight?

At increasing redshift this question is difficult to answer because of the increasing contribution of large-scale structure to the DM budget. It can produce either overdense or underdense lines of sight following some Gaussian distribution about the mean density. In the Local Universe, however, this question is more tractable since the mean IGM contribution and the large-scale structure contributions to the DM budget are minimized. In addition, while the joint distribution $p(z,DM_\mathrm{EG})$ is nontrivial to accurately model\citep{macquart2020census,james2021z}, the discrete structures responsible for DM excesses and deficits may still be identifiable with little or no casework.

This is illustrated qualitatively by the empirically-observed distribution of redshifts and DMs.  We have compiled (Fig.~\ref{fig:macquart}) the vast majority, if not all, FRBs with secure redshifts detected to date with secure redshifts. The 14 host galaxies in this paper significantly improve coverage at low redshifts. Unlike many other FRBs in the Local Universe (e.g.~\citet{bhardwaj2024host}), they have been selected uniformly as a function of total dispersion measure out to total DM $\sim 1000$, at which point the dispersive sweep no longer completely fits in the baseband buffers of CHIME and its outriggers\citep{denman2020gpu,leung2021synoptic,lanman2024chime}.

With the 14 host galaxies in this paper, we assemble a sample of 27 host galaxies at $z < 0.2$. This allows us to make a direct measurement of the distribution of the extragalactic residual DMs, which we refer to as DM$_\textrm{h}$:

$$\textrm{DM}_{h}/(1 + z) = \textrm{DM}_{tot} - \textrm{DM}_\textrm{IGM}(z) - \textrm{DM}_{\textrm{NE2001}} - \textrm{DM}_{\textrm{YT20}}.$$

To capture the width and heavy tail of the DM$_\textrm{h}$ distribution, is often parameterized as a log-normal distribution in the literature with two parameters. $\muhost$ captures the center of the distribution, while its width and heavy tail are both fixed by $\sighost$. While this parametrization is ubiquitous e.g.~\citet{macquart2020census,james2021z,baptista2024measuring} it has no quantitative justification; however the distribution of DM$_\textrm{h}$ is crucial to virtually all attempts to use FRBs as probes of ionized gas. FRBs have been touted as ways to probe the baryon density $\Omega_b$, the IGM gas fraction $f_{IGM}$ and ionization history $f_{ion}$, as well as the Hubble parameter~\citep{james2022measurement}, but all of these parameters are degenerate with the mean of the distribution ($\mu_\textrm{host}$ in the log-normal parameterization). FRBs have also been touted as probes of astrophysical feedback through one-point and two-point cross-correlation analyses~\citep{rafiei_ravandi2021chime,2019arXiv190102418M}; this application requires breaking degeneracies with measurements of the scatter in the DM$_\textrm{h}$ distribution ($\sigma_\textrm{host}$ in the log-normal parameterization)~\citep{mcquinn2014locating,baptista2024measuring}.

In the last section of this paper we combine our low-redshift host sample with FRBs in the literature to characterize the $\dmh$ distribution. We do so non-parametrically, by measuring the moments (i.e. the mean, width, skew, and kurtosis) of the $\dmh$ distribution. We compare our results to the results of global fits~\citep{james2021z,connor2024gas} which use the \textit{de facto} standard log-normal parameterization by translating $\muhost$ and $\sighost$ into the equivalent moments. 

We argue that using the moments of the $\dmh$ distribution adds flexibility in the ``right'' way: it allows the RMS scatter (the variance) in the distribution to be  characterized independently of its heavy tail (i.e. the skew of the distribution). In the future, DM contributions such as the contribution from host galaxy disks~\citep{cassanelli2022fast}, intervening haloes, and the large-scale structure could potentially be disentangled by their distinct imprints on the moments of the $\dmh$ distribution.

In our moment-based analysis, the distribution of $\dmh$ is assumed to have some mean, standard deviation, skew, and kurtosis. We first estimate the mean and standard deviation $M_h$ and $S_h$ using maximum-likelihood estimation with a Gaussian likelihood summed over sightlines $i$: an approach which is simplistic enough for standardization across the field. We maximize

$$\log \mathcal{L}(M_h,S_h) = -\sum_i \dfrac{|\dmh_{,i} - M_h|^2}{2(S_h^2 + \sigma_i^2)}$$

where $\sigma_i^2 = (0.2\mathrm{DM}_\textrm{NE2001,i})^2 + \sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2(z)$ and where $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$ accounts for the redshift-dependent scatter in the DM distribution due to large-scale structure. We fix $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$ to the results of dispersion-measure ray tracing through the Illustris TNG simulation~\citep{walker2024dispersion}, but note that the small redshift range ($z < 0.2$) prevents us from disentangling our fitted parameter $S_h^2$ from $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$, which adds 63.3 pc cm$^{-3}$ of RMS scatter at $z = 0.1$ and 105 pc cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 0.2$. We then plug in our best estimates of $M_h$ and $S_h$ into the standard weighted estimators to estimate the skew and kurtosis $\Gamma_h$ and $\kappa_h$ respectively. Defining weights $w_i = (S_h^2 + \sigma_i)^{-2}$ we have

$$\Gamma_h = \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh_{,i} - M_h)^3}{S_h^{3}\sum_i w_i} $$
$$\kappa_h = \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh_{,i} - M_h)^4}{S_h^{4}\sum_i w_i}.$$

\section{Astrometric Calibration}\label{sec:accuracy}
We localize a total of $100$ single pulses from $18$ distinct, well-localized pulsars. The pulses cover a wide range of detected S/N values ($5 \leq \text{S/N} \leq 100$) and declinations ($+6^\circ \leq \delta \leq +80^\circ$). The Crab pulsar -- PSR B0531+21 -- as well one pulsar below a declination of $\delta < +6^\circ$ are omitted from the sample. Baseband localisations of the Crab are systematically offset by $\sim 2 ~\mathrm{arcmin}$ West of South of CHIME's primary beam. We suspect this offset arises from confusion with the surrounding, radio-bright nebula whose angular extent of roughly $5~\mathrm{arcmin}$ in declination at $5~\mathrm{GHz}$ \citep{Bietenholz_2004} makes it incompletely resolved by the short internal baselines of CHIME. We do not observe this behavior in any other pulsar at similar declinations to the Crab and therefore rule that the Crab is an outlier. We have also identified instances for which pulses originating from PSR B1642-03 are poorly baseband localised for reasons that are not yet entirely well-understood. We suspect that imperfect characterization of CHIME's response to variations in ambient temperature is likely the driving factor at these very-low declinations \citep{michilli2020analysis}. Unfortunately, the lack of bright, single-pulse emitting pulsars between $-3^\circ\leq \delta \leq +6^\circ$ limits our ability to robustly test baseband localisations within this region of CHIME's primary beam. The FRB sample presented in this paper contains one FRB that lies within this region: however, we caution using the associated host to draw any significant conclusions until the systematic effects at low declinations are better understood.\textbf{Only one, maybe two, of our associated host galaxies falls in this range, and the robustness of the association does not change if we increase the declination error (check this). Alternatively, the host galaxy could be downgraded to silver.} As a final cut, we limit the analysis to pulses detected within CHIME's primary beam that have a detected S/N $> 5$. All FRBs presented in this sample obey these criteria. Efforts to extend this analysis to sidelobe detections are currently underway and will be presented in future works. 

For the remaining set of 100 pulses, we plot a summary of the baseband localizations performed to validate the uncertainties along the declination axis in Figure \ref{fig:bb_pulsars}. In the left panel, we plot the measured declination offset along with its associated uncertainty as a function of detection S/N for each pulse in black. We overplot the theoretical uncertainty for an FRB detected at $\nu = 600~\mathrm{MHz}$ at zenith by CHIME in red. This uncertainty increases as the angle from zenith increases ($\sigma_\theta\propto 1/\mathrm{cos}\theta$; \citep{michilli2020analysis}), hence why some values tend away from the theoretical prediction. For the 18 FRBs presented in this paper, we plot their measured S/N and associated decl. uncertainties in blue at a fiducial offset of $-2.5^\circ$ for visual comparison with pulsars in a similar range of S/N values. In the right plot, we plot a histogram of the declination offsets normalised by their uncertainties. Assuming that the baseband localisation pipeline is well-calibrated, we expect the distribution of values to be Gaussian distributed with mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1. We measure a mean of 0.1 and standard deviation of 1.0, suggesting that the data is well described by Gaussian statistics. We overplot the corresponding Gaussian distribution in red. The results of this analysis reinforces that the major axes of the CHIME-KKO localisations are well-calibrated over a wide range of detected S/N and declinations.

Selecting the closest calibrator minimizes the impact of ionospheric and beam variations, but choosing the brightest calibrator can ensure a more robust detection. In both strategies, we could accidentally choose a calibrator that is impacted by an unknown systematic, such as those detected in a sidelobe or confused sources. Since we expect all sources to be affected by similar total instrumental delay, we can mitigate this risk by comparing the delays of all calibrators in the field and identify outliers. We thus adopt the following strategy for calibrator selection --- for all calibrators detected in the field, select those whose measured total delays are within 2~ns of the median delay. From these, choose the calibrator closest in declination to the baseband localization of the FRB.

To compare this with other strategies, we perform localizations using all available calibrators for a set of known pulsars, and compare the offsets of the measured to true positions. Figure~\ref{fig:psr_cal_test} shows the offsets for a set of between 1 and 5 observations of 18 pulsars. Each column of points represents a single observation (i.e., baseband dump), with each point the result of localizing using a different in-beam calibrator. The colored points indicate the results of using the calibrator chosen with each selection criterion -- ``Closest \& Good'' refers to the strategy described in the last paragraph, while ``S/N'' and ``Closest'' refer to using the calibrator with the highest S/N cross-correlation and the closest calibrator in declination, respectively.

For almost all of the calibrators tested, our chosen selection method (green points) ensures offsets are less than 2.5 arcsec.
