\begin{abstract}
We present a new catalog of low-redshift FRBs and their host galaxies, assembled by localizing 81 new bursts to 2$\arcsec$ by $\sim 1'$ accuracy using CHIME and the~\kkoname\ Outrigger. Of these 81 bursts, we use the PATH algorithm to associate them to 19 new host galaxies, and present spectroscopic redshifts for 14. We use new bursts from a previously-reported repeating source (FRB20190303A) to constrain the ionization state of a starbursting pair of interacting galaxies. Two new FRB host galaxies (FRB20230203A and FRB20231206A) are associated with known galaxy clusters, doubling the sample of known galaxy cluster FRBs. The latter is a repeating source with a highly significant ($P_{cc} \sim 10^{-3}$) persistent radio source (PRS) candidate. Finally, we use the full dataset to make the most precise characterization of the host contribution to the DM budget: a key to unlocking the utility of FRBs as cosmic probes. Low-redshift host galaxies are crucial components of FRB cosmology, providing the tightest constraints on host-galaxy contributions to the total DM along the sightline of an FRB~\citep{james2021z,jahnsschindler2023how}. We infer the mean and standard deviation of the host contribution for non-dwarf host galaxies ($M_r > -17$) to be $104\pm 16$ and $113\pm12$ pc cm$^{-3}$; this characterization is the most precise to date and is insensitive to galactic feedback models by virtue of the low redshift ($z \sim 0.1$) of our dataset, which minimizes contamination from the intergalactic medium.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are short-duration radio pulses whose surprising diversity has earned them a reputation as perhaps the most mysterious class of extragalactic transients~\citep{cordes2019fast,petroff2022fast}. While thousands of FRBs have been discovered~\citep{collaboration2021first} spanning several orders of magnitude in luminosity, rise time, burst duration, and activity timescale, their origins remain unknown and their potential as cosmological probes remains largely untapped -- bottlenecked by a lack of robust burst localization and host galaxy identification. 

The host galaxies of FRBs are central to the study of FRB progenitors and their eventual utility as cosmological probes; however previous work on the host galaxies of FRBs has followed in the footsteps of the gamma-ray burst (GRB) community~\citep{bloom2002observed,fong2022comprehensive} by focusing primarily on their role in constraining FRB progenitors. One of the earliest studies of FRB host galaxies used five FRB host galaxies localized by the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder~\citep{macquart2020census} to claim that the FRB-host galaxy offset distribution was compatible with short GRB hosts and supernovae~\citep{heintz2020host,bhandari2020host}. This small sample was gradually expanded to 15~\citet{bhandari2022characterizing} and 23 host galaxies, notably including a significant number of repeating FRBs~\citep{gordon2023demographics}, as well as a volume-limited sample of 18 nearby hosts~\citep{bhardwaj2024host}. Recently, the Deep Synoptic Array has detected tens of FRBs and localized them to their host galaxies~\citep{kocz2019dsa,law2024deep,sharma2024preferential,connor2024gas}, with redshifts ranging from  $z = 0.04$ to $1.3$. In addition the recent completion of the ASKAP Fast Transient incoherent-sum survey~\citep{shannon2024commensal} has added an optically-complete sample of 43 FRBs and 30 redshifts with redshifts ranging from $z = 0.023$ to $1.02$, many of which have been characterized in the sample of~\citet{gordon2023demographics} \citep[see also][]{gordon2023fast}.

This paper expands on these efforts using the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment~\citep[CHIME/FRB,][]{collaboration2018chime} and the~\kkoname\ Outrigger~\citep[KKO,][]{lanman2024chime}, the first of its three outrigger stations which will use very long baseline interferometry (VLBI) to pinpoint FRBs. We localize 81 FRB sources with an accuracy of $\sim 1' \times 2''$ (\S~\ref{sec:localization}), serving as a proof-of-concept of the triggered VLBI technique. We systematically investigate the impact of calibrator choice on astrometric accuracy using a sample of single-pulse localizations from pulsars, finding that on this short baseline, our astrometric accuracy is unaffected by our calibrator choice (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

Despite the moderate accuracy of the single-pulse localizations, 19 of the 81 bursts can confidently be associated with bright, nearby host galaxies, as quantified by a conservative application of the Bayesian host association algorithm PATH~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic} (\S~~\ref{sec:association}) and a detailed simulation of the rate of false-positive associations~\citep{andersen2024inprep}. In \S~\ref{sec:followup} we describe our spectroscopic followup observations, which yield new redshifts for 14 of the 19 hosts, but leave detailed spectral characterization for future work. In \S~\ref{sec:crossmatch} we describe a search for multi-wavelength counterparts.
 
In \S~\ref{sec:sample}, we describe the 19 hosts in the sample, and highlight a few interesting cases therein. These bright galaxies represent ideal, nearby targets for spatially-resolved followup studies ~\citep[e.g.,][]{mannings2021high,woodland2023environments}, for which followup facilities can discover faint optical hosts such as dwarf galaxies, which have been known to host a handful of unusual FRBs~\citep{tendulkar2017host,niu2021repeating}. 

Finally, in \S.~\ref{sec:dmh} we describe our measurement of the host galaxy contribution to the DM budget. All quantities in this work are calculated using the \textit{Planck} cosmology for a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe of  $H_0 = 67.7~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, $\Omega_m=0.31$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.69$ \citep{2020}. 

\section{FRB Detection and Initial Localization}\label{sec:localization}
\subsection{Burst detection and characterization with CHIME}
Between December 9, 2023 and February 10, 2024, CHIME/FRB detected $269$ bright (S/N $> 20$) FRB candidates which were later confirmed by two humans inspecting their dynamic spectra. 
Following the detection and real-time localization, triggers were sent to dump the full field of view at CHIME and KKO  for $\sim 100$ milliseconds. During this time, baseband data were successfully dumped for the majority of these 269 candidates at CHIME. This enabled the interferometric localization using the CHIME core (``baseband localization'') on arcminute scales ~\citep{michilli2021analysis}. In many but not all of these cases, data were also successfully captured at the KKO outrigger ~\citep{lanman2024chime}, which was in a commissioning phase until about September 2023. Using the observing strategy developed in~\citet{leung2021synoptic} we use the baseband dumps at KKO to localize a subsample of 81 bursts to 2-arcsecond accuracy along a roughly east-west axis defined by the orientation of the baseline which depends on the exact declination at which each burst was detected. Due to the smaller collecting area of KKO, this sample of 81 bursts was chosen roughly on the basis of burst brightness at CHIME and includes the brightest FRBs detected by the CHIME core: band-averaged flux densities ranging from 3.9-280 Jy. However, successful localization was subject to the sensitivity of KKO, which varied by a factor of several during the commissioning period; therefore the low-flux cutoff is not well-defined. 

The morphology of these bursts as revealed by CHIME-only dynamic spectra is qualitatively very rich. The Stokes-I waterfall plots for the bursts which have associated host galaxies (see Section~XX) are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wfall}. To check for morphological differences in FRB dynamic spectra, we have ordered the dynamic spectra in decreasing order of peak specific luminosity (Jy Gpc$^2$) implied by their host redshifts (see Sec.~\ref{sec:sample}). We point out that the bursts from the two repeating sources in our sample happen to be the two bursts with the lowest peak specific luminosities, and that this occurs by chance with a probability of $0.02$. This suggests that differences in burst energetics between repeaters and non-repeaters cannot be solely attributed to their narrower bandwidths. However, we defer detailed work on burst properties, e.g. morphological fitting~\citep[][Curtin et al. in prep.]{fonseca2024modeling,sand2024morphology137fastradio},  polarimetry~\citep{mckinven2021polarization}, and scintillometry~\citep{schoen2021scintillation}, for later work. In the dynamic spectra of bursts from known repeaters (FRB 20231204A and FRB 20231128A), we see some indication of the canonical downward-drifting substructure present in the repeating sources~\citep{pleunis2021fast}; we also note that they are in the less-luminous half of our sample: a hint that bursts from repeaters may be less luminous than those of non-repeaters. The baseband data of each burst is downsampled in time to reach a S/N $>$ 5, such that the signal is bright enough to determine the structure maximizing DM \citep{HesselsstructmaxDM} at which the bursts are dedispersed. The time resolution of the bursts are plotted in the top-right corner of each waterfall plot. %\textbf{Vishwangi: how was binning set?}

\subsection{Major-axis localization using CHIME core}
We first localize each burst using the CHIME/FRB baseband localization pipeline, which constrains the north-south extent of the localization ellipse. Two effects limit the astrometric accuracy of the baseband pipeline at the arcminute level: thermal expansion of the focal line, as well as clock offsets between the eastern and western cylinders comprising the CHIME core. These two effects manifest as gradients in the astrometric offset between the localized and true position of known pulsars. They are compensated using auxiliary clock measurements and measurements of the feed temperatures taken from archived environmental monitoring data at the time of the burst arrival. After applying these dynamic corrections, a static astrometric offset is applied~\citep{michilli2021analysis}. This correction centers baseband localizations on the true positions of pulsars. 

To validate the quoted accuracy level of this procedure, we apply it to a sample of about 100 pulsars, finding that the root mean square (RMS) accuracy of the pulsar localizations is consistent with the quoted uncertainties (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}). Follow-up observations and EVN localizations of a handful of CHIME-discovered repeating FRBs, including FRB 20180916A~\citep{collaboration2020periodic,marcote2020repeating}; FRB 20200120E~\citep{bhardwaj2021local,kirsten2022repeating}, FRB 20201124A~\citep{lanman2022sudden,nimmo2022milliarcsecond}, and FRB 20220912~\citep{mckinven2022nine,hewitt2024milliarcsecond}, have further confirmed the localization accuracy.

\subsection{Minor-axis localization using VLBI}
Along the east-west axis we add the KKO station. Due to sparse $uv$ coverage and the transience of the signal, it is critical to realistically assess the magnitude of typical localization uncertainties. To establish the astrometric accuracy of our FRB localizations, we trigger single-pulse voltage dumps on pulsars with interferometrically-determined positions at a variety of declinations and hour angles to measure the distribution of astrometric offsets from their true position (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

The VLBI observations follow the strategy developed in~\citet{leung2021synoptic}: we use full-array baseband dumps to search for bright ($\sim$ 1 Jansky) VLBI calibrators from the Radio Fundamental Catalog (RFC) and VLBA calibrator catalog ~\citep[see e.g.,][ and references therein]{ma1998international,petrov2021wide} within the effective field of the baseband dump ($\sim 200$ deg$^2$). At each station, we formed station beams towards each of the $\sim 200$ VLBI calibrators within the field of view. Following~\citet{andrew2024vlbi}, we placed a single correlator pointing in each station beam and correlated each pointing using the~\texttt{PyFX} correlator~\citep{leung2024vlbi}. Visibilities were saved if the calibrator was detected. We detected up to 15 VLBI calibrators per baseband dump this way. Then, we inspected the uncalibrated delay toward each calibrator. In CHIME-KKO VLBI observations the delays measured towards different calibrators typically agree to within 2 ns of each other, indicating that we do not need direction-dependent calibration at this level of delay accuracy. Calibrators with wildly discrepant delays that do not satisfy this criteria can usually be associated with bright sources whose true location is far from the pointing center~\citep{andrew2024vlbi}. We catch these edge cases during delay calibration by disqualifying calibrators whose delays are not within 2 nanoseconds of the median delay computed over other calibrators in the baseband dump. 

After rejecting these misidentified calibrators, we adopt a uniform calibrator selection strategy for our entire sample. We choose the calibrator that minimizes the target-calibrator separation to minimize the impact of the ionosphere, whose impact has already been shown to be minimal on this 66-kilometer baseline~\citep{lanman2024chime}. However, we find using a sample of pulsar localizations that our astrometric accuracy is insensitive to this choice, and that two other reasonable calibrator selection strategies yield a similar astrometric accuracy (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

After a calibrator is selected, we apply delay and phase-calibration to each FRB in the sample to produce calibrated visibilities. For the majority of bursts in the sample, we were able to robustly measure delays in both polarizations. In the majority of cases the delays were coincident between the XX and YY polarization combinations to within 2 nanoseconds. In this scenario we average the delays between the XX and YY polarizations. In other scenarios the delays disagreed by more than 2 nanoseconds. In every scenario where this occurred, the burst was much more strongly detected in one polarization than the other, where the detection was marginal. We attribute this to the intrinsic polarization angle of the burst and the instrumental sensitivity, particularly at the KKO station. In this scenario we use the delay measured in the brighter of the two polarization hands only (XX or YY). The statistical uncertainty on each delay measurement was much smaller than the systematic uncertainties.

Finally, we use a maximum-likelihood delay-mapping procedure to translate these residual time delays into localization contours (See Eq. 4 in~\citet{marcote2020repeating}; also Eq. A1 in~\citet{leung2024vlbi}). Each VLBI localization contour is combined with the baseband localization ellipse as a prior. The combined contour is convolved with a kernel reflecting a conservative estimate of the localization accuracy. Finally, we calculate ellipse parameters (major/minor axes, orientation angle, and central coordinates) from the posterior probability grid.

To characterize the localization accuracy, we apply this procedure to a sample of $\approx 100$ pulsars. In doing so, we find that CHIME-KKO observations achieve an astrometric accuracy of $< 1\arcsec$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}), except at low declinations where additional RFI at the KKO site increases the overall RMS error. We conservatively prescribe the minor-axis astrometric uncertainty of the whole sample to $2\arcsec$ at the $1\sigma$ level.
\section{Host Association}\label{sec:association}
After localizing each burst with VLBI, we use the default priors in the Probabilistically Associating Transients to their Hosts (PATH) framework~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic} to assign posterior probabilities \pox\ to each catalogued galaxy $O$ given the combined localization region $x$ from baseband and CHIME-KKO VLBI. Each localization region is cross-matched with a widefield galaxy survey. Where possible (55/81 fields), we utilize the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS) to find host galaxy candidates
due to its greater depth. In the remainder of observations (26/81 fields), we default to Pan-STARRS PS1, which covers the entire CHIME/FRB discovery footprint.

Stars are first identified in each field on the basis of their classification in Gaia DR3~\citep{gaia2016gaia, gaia2023gaia} and additional 
survey-specific criteria. For DECaLS, we required
$r > 14$ and that
DECaL\_type not be ``PSF''.
For PS1, we also use the
Probabilistic Classifications of Unresolved Point Sources in PanSTARRS1 catalog 
\citep[PS1-PSC;][]{tachibana2018} and the following
criteria on several catalog values:

\begin{itemize}
    \item rKronRad $ > 0$
    \item $r > 15$ 
    \item $\log_{10} | {\rm rPSFLikelihood} | < -2$
    \item  $\rm iPSFMag - iKronMag\_1 > 0.05$
\end{itemize}
\noindent


After the automated removal of these stars, each galaxy 
candidate in the field was assigned a probability $P(O|x)$ of being the true host of the FRB using 
PATH. Finally, each field was visually inspected to identify unaccounted and/or potential misclassified stars and galaxies. When one of these issues was detected, PATH was run again for that field after appropriate removal or addition of the object. 

%Accurately assigning these posterior probabilities is difficult when combining shallow, widefield archival imaging with a highly sensitive FRB detector such as CHIME. 
It is important to note that a highly sensitive 
FRB detector such as CHIME is likely to discover
FRBs with hosts that are too faint for the wide-field, archival imaging that
we have used.
%In this regime, a significant fraction of bursts originates from beyond the maximum redshift out to which a faint FRB host would be detectable. 
As a point of reference, the faint dwarf host of FRB 20121102A~\citep{tendulkar2017host} would be detectable in the DECaLS $r$-band only out to $z \sim 0.2$, depending on the Galactic extinction along the line of sight. The median DM of our sample (326 pc cm$^{-3}$; see Fig.~\ref{fig:hg_vs_dm}) implies a redshift which significantly exceeds this horizon ($z \approx 0.38$).

The PATH framework accounts for this potential survey depth mismatch by specifying the ``unseen'' probability $P(U)$, which uses the FRB redshift distribution, the galaxy luminosity function, the localization ellipse footprint, and the optical survey depth to calculate the prior probability that the host galaxy of an individual FRB in a survey is too faint to be associated with any detected galaxy with high confidence. We choose values of $P(U)$ for the specific combination of FRBs detected by CHIME on the basis of a detailed simulation by~\citet{andersen2024inprep},
for which mock FRBs are seeded in host galaxies as faint as $m_r = 28$ identified in the Hyper Suprime-Cam Subaru Strategic Survey and subsequently ``re-detected'' in the shallower optical surveys 
used for our FRB host associations. 
This simulation provides a characterization of the fraction of Type-I errors (i.e., falsely claiming a galaxy association where there is none). 
%Finally, a value of $P(U)$ is set fand a threshold value of \pox\ are chosen which minimizes the false positive fraction of associations in the sandbox. 

These simulations indicate that the optimal values of $P(U)$ are $0.32$ for the PanSTARRS fields and $P(U) = 0.15$ for the DECaLS fields. We note that these values are higher than those previously used in 
the literature, e.g.\ $P(U) = 0.1$ is used for Pan-STARRS for the DSA-110 host galaxy sample~\citep{law2024deep}. %and $P(U) = 0.0$ are used in ASKAP-ICS follow-up fields~\citep{shannon2024commensal}.

\section{Spectroscopic Followup}\label{sec:followup}
We conducted spectroscopic follow-up observations for the secure host candidates in our sample with the primary goal of measuring their redshifts. Our host candidates have brightnesses of $16.4 < m_r < 22.7$ and are all at low redshifts ($z < 0.33$), and like the sample presented in~\citet{law2024deep}, are uniformly selected from the Pan-STARRS~\citep{chambers2016pan, magnier2020pan,waters2020pan, magnier2020pan2, magnier2020pan3, flewelling2020pan} and Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS)~\citep{dey2019overview} imaging surveys. We restricted follow-up observations to cases where the host association was considered secure, and those fields with a low Galactic extinction ($E(B-V) < 0.30$) for a total of 13 targets. We also observed the host galaxy of FRB\,20230730A ($P(O|x) = 0.95$) even though it did not strictly pass the extinction criterion ($E(B-V) \sim 0.54$). In cases where the secondary host candidate (the galaxy in the field with the second-highest $P(O|x)$) was comparable in brightness, we overlapped the long-slit with both top host galaxy candidates. For nine host galaxies, we used the Kast Double Spectrograph mounted on the Shane 3-m telescope at Lick Observatory on Mount Hamilton in San Jose, California. For five host galaxies, we used DEIMOS on the Keck II telescope in Mauna Kea, and for one host galaxy we used the Gemini North telescope in Mauna Kea as part of our Long and Large Program (PI: Eftekhari). A summary of these observations is provided in Table~\ref{z_table}.

For all of our spectroscopic observations we used the Python Spectroscopic Data Reduction Pipeline ~\citep[PypeIt, v1.15;][]{prochaska2020pypeit}, which included spectral data reduction, flux calibration, and coadding the spectra. We used standard star observations obtained on each night to generate sensitivity functions, which were used to flux calibrate the spectra. The redshifts were then obtained by manual inspection of the coadded spectra to identify prominent
emission lines. Two or more emission lines were used to confirm each redshift (see Table \ref{z_table}), except for the host galaxy of FRB\,20230730A for which only one line was detectable. We attribute this to the high Galactic extinction along 
this sightline.

\section{Multi-wavelength counterpart cross-matching}\label{sec:crossmatch}
The improved localizations for our sample of bright FRBs is a natural dataset in which to search for multi-wavelength counterparts to FRBs, due to the improved localization (median 1$\sigma$ ellipse size of 113 arcsec$^2$), large sample size (81 bursts), and low redshifts of the 19 bursts in the sample with hosts (median $z \sim 0.1$). Motivated by the discovery of persistent radio counterparts \citep{chatterjee2017direct,niu2021repeating} and the association of a Galactic FRB with a known magnetar~\citep{collaboration2020bright,bochenek2020fast}, we cross-match the localization ellipses with the locations of known gamma-ray bursts, and search for compact PRS for the bursts within our sample. Future work will present a comprehensive search for coincidences between these sources and known transients such as supernovae (Dong et al., in prep.)

\subsection{High-energy counterpart search}
We search for temporal and spatial cross-matches between our 81 FRBs and 2589 long and short GRBs  triggers from the GRBWeb database\footnote{\url{https://user-web.icecube.wisc.edu/\~grbweb_public/}} and the General Coordinates Network\footnote{\url{gcn.nasa.gov}} between 1991 April 21 and 9 September 2024. Using only the GRBs with 1$\sigma$ localizations $<1^\circ$, we do not find any joint spatio-temporal (within 1 week in time and within 3$\sigma$ in space) coincidences. We do find three solely spatial cross-matches between FRBs 20231019A, 20230802A, 20230703A and three GRBs: 970306A, 120323A and 920221A*, respectively. To assess the chance coincidence probability we simulate 1000 GRBs and cross-match each with the FRB sample as done in \citet{Curtin2024}. Each mock sample of GRBs has at least three cross matches, implying a high chance coincidence probability, which is consistent with the large uncertainty regions for these GRBs ($\gtrsim0.5^\circ$).  

\subsection{Persistent Radio Source cross-matching methodology}
Thus far, two FRBs have been confidently associated with PRS\citep{chatterjee2017direct, niu2021repeating}. A third PRS was proposed to be associated with the active repeating FRB\,20201124A \citep{Bruni2024Nature}, but the larger spatial offset relative to the FRB and larger physical size of this PRS makes it different from that of FRB 20121102A and 20190520B, which have been shown to be compact on milliarcsecond scales~\citep{marcote2017repeating,bhandariprs190520}.
We perform a systematic search for unresolved radio sources coincident with the CHIME-KKO FRBs in the Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty cm (FIRST; \citealt{FIRST}) and the NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS; \citealt{NVSS}) at 1.4\,GHz, as well as the Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS; \citealt{VLASS}) at 3\,GHz. For sources detected in either VLASS or FIRST, we also search the positions in the LOFAR Two-metre Sky Survey (LoTSS) Data Release 2 (DR2; \citealt{LoTSS}) at 144\,MHz. In each survey, we searched for radio point sources above the $5\sigma$ detection threshold, using the Canadian Initiative for Radio Astronomy Data Analysis (CIRADA)\footnote{\url{http://cutouts.cirada.ca/}} portal to access the image cutouts. 

In the FIRST and NVSS cutouts, we estimated the noise by computing the RMS flux (i.e., $\sigma$) over the entire 2' $\times$ 2' cutout.  While this can lead to slightly overestimated noise estimates if a particularly dominant radio source is present within the field, we found that this method was sufficient for achieving relatively uniform noise estimates across all fields. In VLASS, cutouts of the imaging residuals allowed for a better noise RMS estimate, which improves the sensitivity of our search and deepens our upper limits on persistent radio emission. 

We identified three potential PRSs detected at $3-5\sigma$ significance in VLASS and discuss each of these sources in \S \ref{sec:FRB20231206A}, \ref{sec:FRB20231128A} and \ref{sec:FRB20230926A}. In addition, for the 15 FRBs with redshifts, we translate the VLASS RMS flux sensitivity into upper limits on the PRS luminosity at the redshift of the FRB at 3\,GHz, as listed in Table \ref{tab:hostderived} (excluding FRB\,20231204A, which had no associated VLASS query). For our upper limits, we did not use FIRST since the majority of the CHIME-KKO localizations fell outside of the FIRST footprint. Similarly, the degraded sensitivity and angular resolution make NVSS less constraining than VLASS. 

Due to the low redshift of our host galaxy sample, the majority of our limits fall at or below the luminosities of PRSs associated with FRB\,20121102A and FRB\,20190520B \citep{chatterjee2017direct, niu2021repeating}, adding to the growing body of evidence that those two sources are rare outliers in the FRB population \citep{Law_2022, law2024deep, sharma2024preferential}. Fainter PRSs may exist below the VLASS detection threshold \citep[e.g.][]{Ibik2024prs}. Alternatively, the extended nature of the VLASS radio source suggests that any extended star formation emission could mask a compact associated AGN if the former is a lot brighter than the latter \citep{Condon92, Condon19}.


Moreover, the potential PRS limits are consistent with those found for a sample of FRB repeaters \citep{Ibik2024prs}, and may suggest intrinsically fainter PRSs when compared to the PRSs associated with FRB\,20121102A and FRB\,20190520B, but similar to the PRS associated with FRB\,20201124A \citep{Bruni2024Nature}. These luminosity limits are also similar to those of the PRS analogs presented by~\citet{Dong2024radiosources}. In the neutron star nebula models (see e.g., \citealt{Kashiyama2017, margalit2018concordance}), the lower luminosities can be explained by factors such as older age, larger nebula radius, or intrinsically lower energy output \citep{Margalit2019nebulae}.

\section{Sources of Interest}\label{sec:sample}
In this section, we highlight some of the interesting sources in our sample. Several bursts in our sample have potential PRS candidates, one of which has a low chance-coincidence probability. Three of our host galaxies are embedded in larger-scale structures: one is in a massive X-ray cluster, while another in a rich optical cluster. A third is a repeating FRB associated with a starbursting galaxy merger. We comment on the rich potential of FRBs to probe these systems.

\subsection{FRB 20231204A: a repeating FRB probing the starburst gas in a galaxy merger}\label{sec:FRB20231204A}
FRB 20231204A is a repeat burst from the source FRB 20190303A, which~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} previously localized to an interacting galaxy pair. In that work, the localization was too large to identify the member galaxy. Here, the updated localization provided by FRB 20231204A indicates that the FRB  originates from the member galaxy to the southwest, which is separated from the northeast member galaxy by only $\sim$ 20 kiloparsecs in projection. Long-slit spectroscopy and Baldwin-Phillips-Terlevich (BPT) diagram classification revealed both galaxies in the interacting system to be star-forming, $\sim L^*$ galaxies; We refer the interested reader to~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute,curtin2024prep,ng2024prep} for further details regarding its spectroscopic characterization, burst morphologies, and polarimetric monitoring respectively.

We follow in the footsteps of~\citet{howk2003ionized,howk2006method}, who combined observations of millisecond pulsars in the globular cluster M3 with far-UV spectroscopy to constrain the ionization fraction along a high Galactic-latitude sightline in the Milky Way. We apply a simpler version of this idea to this FRB sightline, which exits its host galaxy at a high host galactic latitude. 

If the FRB sightline passes through some fraction $f$ of the total HI column density $\Sigma$ towards the FRB -- a naive estimate would have $f = 1/2$ -- then the average ionization fraction can be constrained by combining the host contribution to the DM excess and the HI column:
$$\left(\dfrac{f_{ion}}{0.78}\right)\left(\dfrac{f \Sigma}{10^{21}\textrm{ cm}^{-3}}\right) \lesssim 112\textrm{ pc cm}^{-3}.$$

where the rest-frame host galaxy DM contributio on the right hand side is defined by

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{\dmh}{1 + z_h} = \textrm{DM}_\mathrm{tot} - \langle \textrm{DM}_\textrm{cosmic}(z_h)\rangle - \textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,ISM}} - \textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,halo}}.
\label{eq:dmh_definition}
\end{equation}

Combined with dedicated observations of the neutral gas in this galaxy merger, this could provide the first FRB-derived constraints on the ISM and CGM (circumgalactic medium) of a merging galaxy system.
%It also requires us to assume that the gas of the interstellar medium (ISM) of the host is not displaced significantly between the formation time of the progenitor and the emission of the burst, and that the FRB is located behind an order-one fraction of the ionized gas. Assuming that the delay time between the formation of the progenitor and the emission of the FRB is much smaller than the dynamical timescale of the galaxy merger ($\sim  10^8$ year), the electron column traversed by the FRB can constrain the ionization state of the host galaxy ISM. 

If the HI column density $\Sigma$ were known, the ionization fraction can be constrained using the DM excess of 112 pc cm$^{-3}$ after subtracting Galactic and mean IGM contributions toward this sightline. We roughly establish a lower limit on $\Sigma$ to be $10^{21}\textrm{cm}^{-3}$ based on the association of many FRBs to star-forming hosts, it is plausible that the true position of the FRB is within $\sim 15$ kpc of the center of one of the member galaxies. While archival HI observations are not publicly available for this merging galaxy system,~\citet{tempel2014flux} suggests that it is ten times more luminous than the Milky Way ($2 \times 10^{11} L_\odot$), whose HI column density exceeds $\Sigma \gtrsim 10^{21}$ cm$^{-2}$ out to $15$ kiloparsecs~\citep{kalberla2009hi}.



This exercise could be extended to other FRB hosts in the literature which show some evidence of dynamical  disturbance~\citep{michalowski2021asymmetric,kaur2022fast}. However, the merger activity in those scenarios is not as dramatic as in the case of FRB 20231204A. In the future, a sample of precisely-localized fast radio bursts as well as auxiliary observations characterizing the neutral gas of the star formation site ~\citep[e.g., ][]{leewadell2023host,yamanaka2024alma,glowacki2023wallaby} may be able to discriminate between the ISM and CGM contributions to star formation during galaxy mergers~\citep{sparre2023gas}.

%Hydrodynamical simulations have suggested that starbursts are fueled by inflows of metal-poor gas from the circumgalactic medium when the gas loses angular momentum during a galaxy encounter. However, when galaxies in a two-body encounter pass significantly closer than their virial radii as in the case of FRB 20231204A, the interstellar medium of each constituent galaxy disk gets disturbed, and in these scenarios it has been proposed that gas in the ISM (rather than the CGM) triggers star formation.
\subsection{FRB 20231206A: an FRB behind Abell 576 with an associated PRS candidate}\label{sec:FRB20231206A}
The luminous host galaxy and large dispersion measure excess of FRB 20231206A (295 pc cm$^{-3}$) led us to associate its host galaxy to the galaxy cluster Abell 576~\citep{abell1989catalog}. This X-ray cluster at $z \approx 0.03$ occupies $\sim 9$ square degrees on the sky and hosts over a thousand galaxies. A subset of the galaxies in the direction of the cluster appear at a significantly different redshift~\citep{rines2001infall} than the others; this has led to its identification as an infalling subcomponent with a relative velocity of $\sim 9000$ km/s~\citep{dupke2007merger} relative to the X-ray core of the cluster. The host galaxy of FRB 20231206A -- one of the most luminous in our sample -- is part of this infalling sub-cluster. It has been proposed on the basis of the X-ray morphology of the cluster core that the sub-structure has already made its first passage through the core~\citep{kempner2004chandra} but neither the morphological nor the redshift information can reveal whether the sub-structure is in front or behind the core as viewed from earth.

The DM excess of FRB 20231206A suggests a way to break this degeneracy. At the distance of the cluster center, the host galaxy of FRB 20231206A is transversely displaced by $\approx 5$ Mpc from the cluster center, whose extent is quantified by its $r_{200} = 2.02$ Mpc. At the virial radius the mass of the cluster is between $1-1.4 \times 10^{15} M_\odot$ ~\citep{rines2000infall,girardi1998optical}. We argue that the large (390 pc cm$^{-3}$) DM excess is more consistent with a geometry where the FRB host lies behind the X-ray cluster. In a scenario where the FRB host lies behind the intra-cluster medium, the observed dispersion measure excess then constrains the ionized gas column of the ICM at large impact parameters (in this case, a gas density measurement at 2 $R_{200}$) far beyond the typical reach of X-ray based gas fraction measurements which typically the cluster gas fraction at $\sim R_{2500}-R_{500}$ ~\citep[e.g.,][]{ettori1999rosat, mantz2022cosmological}.
%As in the earlier case, the poor FRB localization limits us to an illustrative order-of-magnitude estiamte. Under the assumption of a cosmological ionization fraction, we estimate $f_{gas} \sim 0.6$ at $\sim 2 R_{vir}$.

This source is coincident with the most probable host galaxy (P(O\textbar$x$) = 0.9897) within the $1\sigma$ localization ellipse. Upon revisiting the corresponding VLASS Quick Look (VLASS-QL) query, we identified a source coincident with the FIRST source with a detection significance of $\sim3.5\sigma$ and luminosity of $L_{3.0~\mathrm{GHz}} = 5\times10^{28}~\mathrm{erg~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Hz}^{-1}$, comparable to those of FRB 20190520B. Extending the method outlined in \cite{Eftekhari18pcc} for our elliptical localization region, we calculated a $P_\mathrm{cc}$ with FRB\,20231206A of $P_\mathrm{cc} \sim 0.1$, and found no associated LoTSS data at the source position.\par

\section{A direct measurement of the host contribution to the DM budget}\label{sec:dmh}
The distribution of of the host galaxy DM contribution (hereafter, \dmh) is crucial to virtually all attempts to forward model FRB dispersion measures as probes of ionized gas. Through ``one-point'' analyses of the joint probability distribution of extragalactic DM as a function of redshift~\citep{james2021z}, FRBs have been touted as excellent probes of the baryon density $\Omega_b$, IGM gas properties, and even the Hubble constant; yet these quantities are often degenerate with host galaxy properties in models ~\citep[e.g. Figure 9 of][]{james2022measurement}.  Two-point cross-correlation analyses are more robust; yet these cross-correlation analyses are forecasted to be sensitive to the noise added by host galactic contamination of DM measurements~\citep{mcquinn2014locating,rafiei_ravandi2021chime,2019arXiv190102418M}.

Individual DM budgets have been observed to both undershoot ~\citep[FRB 20220319B; see][]{ravi2023deep} and overshoot expectations due to an insufficiently-modeled sightline~\citep[e.g. FRB 20190520A][]{niu2021repeating,lee2023frb}. With every new FRB, observers are motivated to dissect in great detail~\citep{2024arXiv240200505K} the origin of the DM excess after subtracting DM$_\text{cosmic}$: is the remainder dominated by a dense circum-source medium, or could it possibly originate from gas along the line of sight?

At $z \gtrsim 0.2$ this question is difficult to answer because the contribution of large-scale structure to the DM budget -- the overdensities/underdensities relative to $\langle \textrm{DM}_\text{cosmic}\rangle$ -- dominates over smaller-scale contributions from e.g. the host galaxy and circum-source medium. While estimates are highly uncertain,~\citet{walker2024dispersion} estimates the RMS fluctuation of the cosmic contribution to be $\sim 100$ pc cm$^{-3}$ -- roughly the size of the host contribution -- at $z = 0.2$. Though notable exceptions exist, this is enough to swamp the DM contribution from most circum-source media and Milky Way-like electron disks at most inclination angles. At lower redshifts, this question is more tractable since the mean cosmic contribution 
%and the large-scale structure contributions 
to the DM budget is minimized. Above a DM threshold ($\sim 200$ pc cm$^{-3}$ in this case), the discrete structures responsible for DM excesses and deficits may be identifiable in archival surveys with little or no casework. In Fig.~\ref{fig:host_dm} we summarize our efforts to identify sources of large excess DM.

As seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:macquart}, the large localization regions resolution enable associations with bright, low-redshift host galaxies, whose areal density is sufficiently low to enable secure host association. We have compiled the vast majority of FRBs with secure redshifts and publicly-available photometry detected to date, using the host galaxies in this paper to assemble a sample of 49 host galaxies at $z < 0.2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:macquart}). This allows us to make the most precise measurement of the distribution of \dmh (see Eq.~\ref{eq:dmh_definition}).

with $z_h$ the redshift of the host galaxy and where the Milky Way disk and halo contributions  $\textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,ISM}}$ and $\textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,halo}}$ are estimated using the NE2001~\citep{cordes2002ne2001} and~\citet{yamasaki2020galactic} models respectively. The cosmic contribution is calculated using the \texttt{igm.average\_DM} function in the widely-used open source \hyperlink{https://github.com/FRBs/FRB/blob/40c371bdebcf0624509f6f9f8d47d9cc815c8d15/frb/dm/igm.py\#L247}{FRB} repository, which implements the standard formula for the cosmic DM using the cosmic baryon budget~\citep[e.g.][]{fukugita1998cosmic}, which implies $f_
\textrm{diffuse} = 0.844$ which over our redshift range is constant to within 0.001.

In principle, \dmh\ is contaminated by contributions from all line-of-sight structures, but at low redshifts it is likely to be dominated by contributions from the host galaxies of FRBs. It is often parameterized by modeling it probilistically as a log-normal distribution with a mean $\muhost$ and width $\sighost$. This parametrization lacks physical justification, yet is ubiquitous \citep[e.g.,][]{macquart2020census,james2021z,baptista2024measuring}.

To improve on this previous work, we combine our low-redshift host sample with FRBs in the literature to characterize the $\dmh$ distribution. Rather than fitting a log-normal distribution we use a simpler non-parametric method, where we measure the moments (i.e., the mean, width, skew, and excess kurtosis) of the $\dmh$ distribution. Using the moments of the $\dmh$ distribution captures crucial information that the log-normal distribution may not, since it allows the RMS scatter (the variance) in the distribution to be characterized independently of its heavy tail, i.e. its skew. Effects such as scattering and propagation through the inclined disks of FRB host galaxies will modify the moments of the $\dmh$; in the future the locations of FRBs within their host galaxies could potentially be disentangled by their distinct imprints on the moments of the $\dmh$ distribution.

We first summarize all available data on the $\dmh$ distribution in Figs.~\ref{fig:macquart} and~\ref{fig:host_dm}. Next, we perform a redshift cut ($z < 0.2$), andexclude from our analysis non-luminous ($M_r > -17$) and dwarf hosts \citep[FRB 20121102A, FRB 20190520B, and FRB 20210117A; ][]{bhandari2023nonrepeating}. These sources are not representative from the population of detected FRBs; on the basis of the high specific star formation rates of their hosts and persistent radio source counterparts their progenitors may be produced by a formation channel distinct from that of FRBs from more massive galaxies. For this reason, our \dmh\ parameter estimates are valid only for FRBs with non-dwarf hosts over the aforementioned range of host stellar masses. Finally, we identify the following critical subsamples of FRBs within the remaining 49 FRBs:
\begin{enumerate} 
\item We separately consider FRBs (all from CHIME/FRB) for which the low total DM was crucial to making a host galaxy association because of the explicit selection bias on \dmh~\citep[8 bursts from ][]{bhardwaj2024host,michilli2023subarcminute,Ibik2024host}. Analyzing this sub-sample separately confirms that these bursts are drawn from the low-\dmh\ tail of the FRB population.
\item We also separately considerisolate galaxy cluster FRBs, since these have significant contamination from outside their host galaxy. These include FRB 20220914A and FRB 20220509G~\citep{connor2023deep}, FRB 20231206A (\S~\ref{sec:FRB20231206A}), FRB 20230203A (\S~\ref{sec:FRB20230203A}), and FRB 20231204A (\S~\ref{sec:FRB20231204A}). While we have not performed a complete search, we note that clusters account for a significant fraction of the largest outliers ($\dmh > 200$ pc cm$^{-3}$) observed in our sample, suggesting that we are complete to associated clusters whose foreground DM contribution exceeds this threshold.
\item We separately consider edge-on host galaxies, for which we report inclinations where available. These include FRB 20210603A \citep[$i \sim 83^\circ$; ][]{cassanelli2024fast}, FRB 20220207C \citep[$i \sim 74-80^\circ$;][]{law2024deep,bhardwaj2024selection}, and two FRBs from this work: FRB 20230203A ($i \sim 77-81^\circ$) and FRB 20231005A ($i \sim 81^\circ$), for which we apply the InclinationZoo estimator~\citep{kourkchi2020cosmicflows} to publicly-available SDSS and Pan-STARRS imaging, verifying that that measurements are robust between both images. Three remaining FRBs have no quantitative estimates readily available, but their host galaxies appear highly elongated in published images justifying their classification as edge-on: FRB 20231120A~\citep{2024arXiv240916964S}, and FRB 20240201A and FRB 20240310A from~\citet{shannon2024commensal}. 
\end{enumerate}

We then measure the first four moments of the $\dmh$ distribution using our full dataset of $z < 0.2$ FRBs as well as the three aforementioned subsamples (low-DM, edge-on, and galaxy cluster FRBs). The four moments are defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:mean}-\ref{eq:kurt}. In all cases, each data point is weighted to account for errors in the NE2001 model and an RMS contribution from the cosmic web. In addition, we de-bias the kurtosis estimate (by subtracting 3) such that a Gaussian distribution of \dmh\ values has $\kappa_h = 0$. %$\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$ to the results of dispersion-measure ray tracing through the Illustris TNG simulation~\citep{walker2024dispersion}, but note that the small redshift range ($z < 0.2$) prevents us from disentangling our fitted parameter $S_h^2$ from $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$, which adds 63.3 pc cm$^{-3}$ of RMS scatter at $z = 0.1$ and 105 pc cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 0.2$. 
.

\begin{align} 
\langle \dmh\rangle &= \dfrac{\sum_i w_i \dmh{}_{,i}}{\sum_i w_i} \label{eq:mean}\\
S_h^2 &= \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh{}_{,i} - M_h)^2}{\sum_i w_i}\label{eq:std} \\
\Gamma_h &= \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh{}_{,i} - M_h)^3}{S_h^{3}\sum_i w_i} \label{eq:skew}\\
\kappa_h &= \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh{}_{,i} - M_h)^4}{S_h^{4}\sum_i w_i} - 3.\label{eq:kurt}
\end{align}

The weights are defined to be $w_i \propto \sigma_i^{-2}$ with $$\sigma_i^2 = (0.2\text{DM}_\textrm{NE2001,i})^2 + (10~\text{pc cm}^{-3})^2 + \sigma_\textrm{cosmic}^{2}.$$ This formula accounts for 20\% uncertainties in the Milky Way disk model, a constant uncertainty in the MW halo model, and an RMS scatter in the cosmic dispersion measure originating from haloes as well as the IGM. We fix $\sigma_\textrm{cosmic} = 80~\text{pc cm}^{-3}$ in order to be roughly consistent with estimates from~\citet{walker2024dispersion}. While it is difficult to separate the RMS scatter in the IGM from $S_h$ we find that our results are insensitive to the particular value of $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}$ used, since this choice only affects the relative weighting of the data. Choosing $\sigma_\textrm{IGM} = 0$ shifts the final parameter estimates by at most $5\%$ of our quoted measurement uncertainty, which was estimated by bootstrapping each sample with replacement 4000 times. 

Our final parameter estimates for our full sample and various sub-samples are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:mvsk} and Table~\ref{tab:mvsk}. Measurements from our full sample are the most precise ($\sim 10\%$) estimates to date and are insensitive to galactic feedback or a particular functional form of the distribution. In addition, measurements are performed independently for the low-DM, edge-on, and galaxy cluster subsamples. Finally, these three sets of FRBs are removed from the full sample in an attempt to define a ``clean'' subsample for cosmological measurements. In particular, we expect that relative to the full sample, $\langle\dmh\rangle$ for the ``clean'' subsample will be less biased, and that $S_h$ will be reduced. While each of these samples have very different $\dmh$, they roughly counterbalance each other in terms of the overall effect on $\dmh$. We see that $S_h$ is also largely unaffected by this change.


We can compare our measurements to existing measurements of the host galaxy contribution \citep{james2021z,shin2023inferring,connor2024gas} using analytical expressions which translate the log-normal distribution into non-log space. We compare only the mean, i.e. $\langle \dmh\rangle$ because of each methods' different assumptions. First, the number of bursts cannot be directly translated, since in global fitting analyses, low-redshift bursts constrain the host contribution, whereas high-redshift bursts mainly constrain the IGM. In addition, unlocalized and localized FRBs have vastly different constraining power.

The scatter and tail of the distribution cannot be directly compared either, since in those analyses, the RMS host galaxy contribution is divided into host-related and cosmic contributions using an assumed redshift dependence of the latter. In contrast, our measurement makes no assumptions about the redshift dependence but does not allow for the IGM and host contributions to $S_h$ to be separated.

We see no strong trend when binned by host luminosity, nor when dividing the sample by detection experiment; but sightlines with galaxy clusters and edge-on host galaxies, as well as FRBs selected on the basis of their total DM have significantly different values of $\langle\dmh\rangle$: a warning sign for attempts to simplistically model the host galaxy contribution. 

First, we split our sample based on the median $M_r$. The $\langle\dmh \rangle$ distribution for host galaxies within the ranges $-23 > M_r > -20.6$ and $-20.6 > M_r > -17$ differ by $\approx 30$ pc cm$^{-3}$, a $\sim 1\sigma$ effect.

Second, we find that relative to the parent sample (``All''), FRBs in groups, clusters and edge-on host galaxies have much higher values of $\langle \dmh\rangle$ ($> 100$ pc cm$^{-3}$) but similar values of $S_h$ to that of the parent sample. This implies that these systems will bias all cosmological analyses which are sensitive to $\langle\dmh\rangle$.

Third, we find that low-DM selected FRBs have a \dmh\ distribution with much lower mean and standard deviation from the others, implying they should be omitted from population-level cosmological analyses spanning a wide range of redshifts, unless accounted for in a model of the redshift evolution of $\langle\dmh\rangle$. 

Finally, we sub-divide the clean sample into ASKAP/ICS, DSA-110, and CHIME/FRB subsamples (excluding low-DM FRBs for the latter) to check for biases in $\langle\dmh\rangle$ as a function of detection instrument. Each subsample is in agreement at the level of 1$\sigma$ from the others; each subsample is also in agreement with global fitting analyses conducted on datasets from each detection experiment, e.g. our sample consisting of bursts detected in the ASKAP/Incoherent Sum Survey (ASKAP/ICS) agrees with the analysis in~\citet{james2021z}, which is dominated by ASKAP/ICS sightlines. Similarly, we see consistency at the 1$\sigma$ level between~\citet{connor2024gas} and our assembled DSA-110 sub-sample. Finally, the CHIME/FRB only sample dramatically improves the constraining power relative to the conclusions of~\citet{shin2023inferring}.

The selection effects relevant to this measurement are likely modest. The CHIME/FRB baseband system directly selects against high DM bursts, but this effect is only present when the total DM $> 1000$ pc cm$^{-3}$, and is unlikely to affect our measurement~\citet{collaboration2021first,shin2023inferring}. The CHIME/FRB real-time search pipeline is strongly affected by nonstationary noise, which translates into an lower detection probability for low-flux, highly-scattered bursts. While this could affect the bursts that are detected, we note that bright, highly-scattered bursts are nevertheless detectable~\citep{shin2024investigating}, and that the bursts bright enough to be detected in VLBI should not be strongly subject to this selection effect. We strongly select on host luminosity, because of our localization region, which is usually larger than typical FRB host galaxies. Hence, we caution that our results are only applicable for non-dwarf host galaxies brighter than $M_r < -17$.

\section{Conclusion}
The challenge of localizing FRBs and associating them to their host galaxies has been a bottleneck in the ongoing effort to understand their origins. In this paper, we have demonstrated  the viability of wide-field VLBI on FRBs on a large sample of bursts, using data from the first operational CHIME Outrigger KKO~\citep{lanman2024chime}. We use a VLBI observation strategy and a calibrator grid developed in prior papers~\citep{leung2021synoptic,andrew2024vlbi} to present several-arcsecond accuracy localizations for 81 sources. We secure host galaxy associations for 19 sources and new spectroscopic redshifts for 14, many of which are at low redshift $(z < 0.2)$. 

These low-redshift galaxies are rich targets for understanding the origin of FRBs. The two repeating sources in our sample are associated with potential PRS candidates in archival data. One source presented separately from this paper (FRB 20240209A) displays a large offset from its massive elliptical host; see papers in prep by Shah et al. (2024) and Eftekhari et al. (2024). Towards other sightlines, upper limits on persistent radio counterparts challenge the existence of PRS at or below the luminosity of known PRS. In addition, at low redshifts, the imprint of the IGM is minimized. This allows us to identify sightlines with high excess dispersion, which in many cases can be attributed to a nearby galaxy cluster or an edge-on host geometry. We use this additional statistical power to directly measure the enigmatic host galaxy contribution to FRB dispersion measures in a way that is agnostic to any particular parameterization of feedback and fluctuations in the IGM; we measure $\langle\dmh\rangle = 104\pm16$ and $S_h = 113\pm12$ for non-dwarf hosts with $M_r < -17$. Our measurement represents a significant improvement relative to global fitting methods that use a combination of sources at high and low redshifts to break degeneracies under the assumption of a particular redshift evolution of the host galaxy contribution. Model-insensitive measurements of $\dmh$ like the one here will be a crucial anchor for FRB cosmology at higher redshifts, and contain rich information about the spatial distribution of FRBs within their hosts. More broadly, the observational techniques for FRB detection and localization, host galaxy association, and follow-up pave the way for upcoming large-scale FRB surveys~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian,hallinan2019dsa}, which will revolutionize our understanding of FRBs and the universe they illuminate.

\section{Astrometric Calibration}\label{sec:accuracy}
\subsection{Baseband Localization: Accuracy of Quoted Declination Uncertainties}
We localize a total of $100$ single pulses from $18$ distinct, well-localized pulsars. The pulses cover a wide range of detected S/N values ($5 \leq \text{S/N} \leq 100$) and declinations ($+6^\circ \leq \delta \leq +80^\circ$). The Crab pulsar -- PSR B0531+21 -- as well one pulsar below a declination of $\delta < +6^\circ$ are omitted from the sample. Baseband localisations of the Crab are systematically offset by $\sim 2 ~\mathrm{arcmin}$ West of South of CHIME's primary beam. We suspect this offset arises from confusion with the surrounding, radio-bright nebula whose angular extent of roughly $5~\mathrm{arcmin}$ in declination at $5~\mathrm{GHz}$ \citep{Bietenholz_2004} makes it incompletely resolved by the short internal baselines of CHIME. We do not observe this behavior in any other pulsar at similar declinations to the Crab and therefore conclude that the Crab is an outlier. We have also identified instances for which pulses originating from PSR B1642-03 are poorly baseband localised for reasons that are not yet entirely well-understood. We suspect that imperfect characterization of CHIME's response to variations in ambient temperature is likely the driving factor at these very-low declinations \citep{michilli2021analysis}. Unfortunately, the lack of bright, single-pulse emitting pulsars in the range $-3^\circ\leq \delta \leq +6^\circ$ limits our ability to robustly test baseband localisations within this region of CHIME's primary beam. The FRB sample presented in this paper contains one FRB that lies within this region. As a final cut, we limit the analysis to pulses detected within CHIME's primary beam that have a detection (``L4'') signal-to-noise ratio of $> 5$ (all FRBs presented in this paper sample obey these criteria).

For the remaining set of 100 pulses, we plot a summary of the baseband localizations performed to validate the uncertainties along the declination axis in Figure \ref{fig:bb_pulsars}. In the left panel, we plot the measured declination offset along with its associated uncertainty as a function of detection S/N for each pulse in black. We overplot the theoretical uncertainty for an FRB detected at $\nu = 600~\mathrm{MHz}$ at zenith by CHIME in red. This uncertainty increases as the angle from zenith increases~\citep[$\sigma_\theta\propto 1/\mathrm{cos}\ \theta$][]{michilli2021analysis}; hence some values tend away from their theoretical prediction. For the 19 FRBs presented in this paper, we plot measured S/N and associated decl. uncertainties in blue at a fiducial offset of $-2.5^\circ$ for visual comparison with pulsars with a similar range of S/N values. In the right panel, we plot a histogram of the declination offsets normalised by their uncertainties. Assuming that the baseband localisation pipeline is well-calibrated, we expect the distribution of values to be Gaussian distributed with mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1. We measure a mean of 0.1 and standard deviation of 1.0, suggesting that the data is well described by Gaussian statistics. We overplot the corresponding Gaussian distribution in red. The results of this analysis reinforce that the major axes of the CHIME-KKO localisations are well-calibrated over a wide range of detected S/N and declinations.

\subsection{VLBI Localization: Declination-dependent astrometric accuracy and impact of Calibrator Selection}
We perform a similar validation check of our VLBI localization procedure, using 62 single pulse localizations of 18 distinct pulsars at a variety of declinations (see Fig.~\ref{fig:psr_cal_test}). Here, we use the abundance of astrometric calibrators from the Radio Fundamental Catalogue and VLBA calibrator grid~\citep{ma1998international,petrov2021wide} at 600 MHz~\citep{andrew2024vlbi} to explore the impact of the often ad-hoc choice of VLBI calibrator on the resulting astrometry, finding small variations in the sample-averaged RMS localization accuracy. This result is encouraging for next-generation FRB telescopes, which will rely on similar techniques to pinpoint FRBs: these include CHIME's successor CHORD~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian}, as well as other compact arrays being built as HIRAX~\citep{newburgh2016hirax}, BURSTT~\citep{lin2022burstt}, and CASPA~\citep{luo2024fast}.  

For instance, selecting the closest calibrator minimizes the impact of ionospheric and beam variations, but choosing the calibrator that maximizes the S/N of the target after applying delay and phase solutions can ensure a more robust detection of the FRB. However, under either rule, it is possible to choose a calibrator that is impacted by an unknown systematic, such as those detected in a sidelobe or confused sources. Since we expect all sources to have a similar total instrumental delay, we can mitigate this risk by comparing the delays of all calibrators in the field, which we use to identify outliers. We thus adopt the following strategy for calibrator selection: for all calibrators detected in the field, we select those whose measured total delays are within 2~ns of the median delay. From these, we choose the calibrator closest in declination to the baseband localization of the FRB.

To compare this with other strategies, we perform localizations using all detected VLBI calibrators for a set of pulsars with known positions, and compare the residuals between the measured and true positions. Figure~\ref{fig:psr_cal_test} shows the offsets for a set of between 1 and 5 observations of 18 pulsars. For a fixed x-coordinate on the plot, each column of points represents a single observation (i.e., baseband dump). Each point in the column represents the measured astrometric offset for a particular in-beam calibrator. The gray points are the calibrators which were not chosen, while the colored points indicate the offsets measured with the calibrator chosen using one of three selection criteria. -- ``Closest \& Good'' refers to the strategy described in the previous paragraph, while ``S/N'' and ``Closest'' refer to using the calibrator which maximizes the S/N of the phase-referenced target visibilities, and the closest calibrator on the sky in declination, respectively.
Fig.~\ref{fig:psr_offsets} shows the offset distribution for pulsars localized as a function of which strategy is used. Their RMS values are quite similar, with a few outliers originating from pulsars at low declinations. 

We regard the associations in this paper as highly secure on the basis of the localization accuracy demonstrated in this appendix, in light of the $\sim10\%$ Type-I error rate for the adopted PATH threshold. In addition, we have inflated our astrometric error bars ($\approx 1.5''$ for all declinations, $\approx 0.93''$ excluding low declinations) to $2''$ for the whole sample. We note that any individual host in the sample may be misidentified with the magnitude-dependent false-positive rate of $\sim 10\%$ as described in \S~\ref{sec:association}; the small number of low-declination systems (only FRB 20231123A and FRB 20230222A fall at declinations lower than $21^\circ$) also are slightly more subject to astrometric errors than the rest. The combined effect of astrometric errors, however, are most likely subdominant to the host association false-positive rate; the effect on population-level statistics of the full sample is likely to be minimal.
