\begin{abstract}
Out of 269 bright (S/N $> 20$) fast radio bursts detected by the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) between December 2022 and February 2024, we present 81 fast radio bursts localized to 2'' by $\sim 1'$ accuracy. Of these 81 bursts, we use the PATH algorithm to associate them to 19 new host galaxies, and present spectroscopic redshifts for 14. Previous work on the host galaxies of FRBs has focused on their role in constraining FRB progenitors; in this paper we analyze the host contribution to the DM in a low-redshift ($z \sim 0.1$) dataset where contamination from the intergalactic medium (IGM) is minimized. We use bursts from a previously-reported repeating source to constrain the ionization state of a starbursting pair of interacting galaxies. Two new host galaxies are associated with known galaxy clusters, doubling the sample of known galaxy cluster FRBs; in addition we report two persistent radio source candidates associated with repeating FRBs. Finally, we use the full dataset to make the most precise measurement of the host contribution to the DM budget: a much-needed measurement to establish the utility of FRBs as cosmic probes. Using a new nonparametric method which is independent of any assumed model for galactic feedback, we infer the mean and standard deviation of the host contribution to be $104\pm 16$ and $113\pm12$ pc cm$^{-3}$, respectively.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are short-duration radio pulses whose shocking diversity has earned them a reputation as perhaps the most mysterious class of astrophysical transients~\citep{cordes2019fast,petroff2022fast}. While thousands of FRBs have been discovered~\citep{collaboration2021chime} spanning several orders of magnitude in luminosity, rise time, burst duration, and activity timescale, their origins remain unknown and their potential as cosmological probes remains untapped -- bottlenecked by a lack of robust burst localization and host galaxy identification. 

The host galaxies of FRBs are central to the study of FRB progenitors and their eventual utility as cosmological probes. Following in the footsteps of the gamma-ray burst (GRB) community~\citep{bloom2002observed,fong2022comprehensive},
several early studies have been conducted on FRB host galaxies. With a handful of bursts (5), early studies of sources discovered by the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder~\citep{macquart2020census} found an offset distribution compatible with short GRB hosts and supernovae~\citep{heintz2020host,bhandari2020host}. This small sample was expanded by ~\citet{bhandari2022characterizing} and~\citet{bhardwaj2024host} to 15 and 18 hosts, respectively. Most recently~\citet{law2024deep} and~\citet{sharma2023deep} presented evidence for a broad diversity of delay times and formation channels using a sample of 11 brand-new FRB host galaxies discovered and localized by the Deep Synoptic Array~\citep{kocz2019dsa,law2024deep}.

These initial studies have all been limited due to the small sample sizes involved. However, we are gradually entering an era of of precise FRB localizations and plentiful host galaxy associations: the Deep Synoptic Array has detected tens of FRBs and localized them to their host galaxies~\citep{connor2024gas,sharma2024preferential}, with redshifts ranging from  $z = 0.04$ to $1.3$.
The recent completion of the ASKAP Fast Transient incoherent-sum survey~\citep{shannon2024commensal} has added an optically-complete sample of 43 FRBs and 30 redshifts with redshifts ranging from $z = 0.023$ to $1.02$, many of which have been characterized in the sample of~\citet{gordon2023demographics} \citep[see also][]{gordon2023fast}).

This paper expands on these efforts using the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME/FRB;~\citet{collaboration2018chime}) and KKO~\citep{lanman2024chime}, the first of its three VLBI outrigger stations. Using triggered VLBI, we localize 81 FRB sources with an accuracy of $\sim 1' \times 2''$ (\S~\ref{sec:localization}), serving as a proof-of-concept of the triggered VLBI technique. The abundance of VLBA and RFC astrometric calibrators at 600 MHz which we observe is encouraging for next-generation FRB telescopes which will rely on similar techniques to pinpoint FRBs: these include CHIME's successor CHORD~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian}, as well as other compact arrays being built as HIRAX~\citep{newburgh2016hirax}, BURSTT~\citep{lin2022burstt}, and CASPA~\citep{luo2024fast}. We systematically investigate the impact of calibrator choice on astrometric accuracy using a sample of single-pulse localizations from pulsars, finding that on this short baseline, our astrometric accuracy is unaffected by our calibrator choice (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

Despite the moderate accuracy of the single-pulse localizations, 19 of the 81 bursts can confidently be associated with bright, nearby host galaxies, as quantified by a conservative application of the Bayesian host association algorithm PATH~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic} (\S~~\ref{sec:association}) and a detailed simulation of the rate of false-positive associations~\citep{andersen2024inprep}.

Finally, in \S~\ref{sec:sample}, we describe the 19 hosts in the sample, and highlight a few interesting cases therein. They have brightnesses of $16.4 < m_r < 22.7$ and are all at low redshifts ($z < 0.33$), and like the sample presented in~\citet{law2024deep}, are uniformly selected from the Pan-STARRS~\citep{chambers2016pan, magnier2020pan,waters2020pan, magnier2020pan2, magnier2020pan3, flewelling2020pan} and Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS)~\citep{dey2019overview} imaging surveys. These bright galaxies represent ideal, nearby targets for spatially-resolved followup studies ~\citep[e.g.,][]{mannings2021high,woodland2023environments}, where followup facilities can discover faint optical hosts such as dwarf galaxies, which have been known to host a handful of unusual FRBs~\citep{tendulkar2017host,niu2021repeating}. Low-redshift host galaxies are also crucial components of FRB cosmology, providing the tightest constraints on host-galaxy contributions to the total DM along the sightline of an FRB~\citep{james2021z,jahnsschindler2023how}. 

We perform spectroscopic followup using a combination of the 3-meter Shane Reflector at Lick Observatory, our Long and Large Program at Gemini North (PI: Eftekhari), and observations with the Deep Imaging Multi-Object Spectrograph (DEIMOS) mounted on the Keck II telescope at Mauna Kea, Hawaii. In this paper we present spectroscopic redshifts for 14 of the 19 hosts towards low-extinction sightlines ($E(B-V) < 0.3$) through the Milky Way, and reserve detailed spectral characterization for future work. All quantities in this work are calculated using the \textit{Planck} cosmology for a flat $\Lambda$CDM universe of  $H_0 = 67.7~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, $\Omega_M=0.31$ and $\Omega_\mathrm{vac} = 0.69$ \citep{2020}. 

\section{FRB Detection and Initial Localization}\label{sec:localization}
\subsection{Initial burst detection at CHIME}
Between December 9, 2023 and Feb 10, 2024, CHIME/FRB detected $269$ bright (S/N $> 20$) FRB candidates which were later confirmed by two human tsars inspecting their dynamic spectra. 
Following the detection and real-time localization, triggers were sent to dump the full field of view at CHIME and KKO  for $\sim 100$ milliseconds. During this time, baseband data were successfully dumped for the majority of these 269 candidates at CHIME. This enabled the interferometric localization using the CHIME core (``baseband localization'') on arcminute scales ~\citep{michilli2020analysis}. In many but not all of these cases, data were also successfully captured at the KKO outrigger ~\citep{lanman2024chime}, which was in a commissioning phase until about June 2023. We use the baseband dumps at CHIME and KKO to localize these bursts to 2-arcsecond scale precision along a roughly east-west axis defined by the orientation of the baseline. The localization ellipses are thus highly elongated along a roughly east-west axis, whose orientation angle depends on the exact declination at which each burst was detected.

\subsection{Major-axis localization using CHIME core}
We first localize each burst using the CHIME/FRB baseband localization pipeline, which constrains the north-south extent of the localization ellipse. Two effects limit the astrometric accuracy of the baseband pipeline at the arcminute level: thermal expansion of the focal line, as well as clock offsets between the eastern and western cylinders comprising the CHIME core. These two effects manifest as gradients in the astrometric offset between the localized and true position of known pulsars. They are compensated using auxiliary clock measurements and measurements of the feed temperatures taken from archived environmental monitoring data at the time of the burst arrival. After applying these dynamic correction, a static astrometric offset is applied~\citep{michilli2020analysis}. This correction centers baseband localizations on the true positions of pulsars. 

To validate the quoted accuracy level of this procedure, we apply it to a sample of about 100 pulsars, finding that the root mean square (RMS) accuracy of the pulsar localizations is consistent with the quoted uncertainties (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}). Follow-up observations and EVN localizations of a handful of CHIME-discovered repeating FRBs including FRB 20180916A~\citep{collaboration2020periodic,marcote2020repeating}; FRB 20200120E~\citep{bhardwaj2021local,kirsten2022repeating}, FRB 20201124A~\citep{lanman2022sudden,nimmo2022milliarcsecond}, and FRB 20220912~\citep{mckinven2022nine,hewitt2024milliarcsecond} have further confirmed the localization accuracy.

\subsection{Minor-axis localization using VLBI}
Along the east-west axis we add the KKO station. Due to sparse $uv$ coverage and the transience of the signal, it is critical to realistically assess the magnitude of typical localization uncertainties. To establish an astrometric accuracy, we trigger single-pulse voltage dumps on pulsars at a variety of declinations and hour angles (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}). 

The VLBI observations follow the strategy developed in~\citet{leung2021synoptic}: we use full-array baseband dumps to search for bright ($\sim$ 1 Jansky) VLBI calibrators from the Radio Fundamental Catalog (RFC) and VLBA calibrator catalog ~\citep[see, e.g.,][ and references therin]{ma1998international,petrov2021wide} within the effective field of the baseband dump ($\sim 200$ deg$^2$). At each station, we formed station beams towards each of the $\sim 200$ VLBI calibrators within the field of view. Following~\citet{andrew2024vlbi}, we placed a single correlator pointing in each station beam and correlated each pointing using the~\texttt{PyFX} correlator~\citep{leung2024vlbi}. Visibilities were saved if the calibrator was detected. We detected up to 15 VLBI calibrators per baseband dump this way. Then, we inspected the uncalibrated delay toward each calibrator. In CHIME-KKO VLBI observations the delays measured towards different calibrators typically agree to within 2 ns of each other, indicating that we do not need direction-dependent calibration at this level of delay accuracy. Calibrators with wildly discrepant delays that do not satisfy this criteria can usually be associated with bright sources whose true location is far from the pointing center~\citep{andrew2024vlbi}. We catch these edge cases during delay calibration by disqualifying calibrators whose delays are not within 2 nanoseconds of the median delay computed over other calibrators in the baseband dump. 

After rejecting these misidentified calibrators, we adopt a uniform calibrator selection strategy for our entire sample. We choose the calibrator that minimizes the target-calibrator separation to minimize the impact of the ionosphere, whose impact has already been shown to be minimal on this 66-kilometer baseline~\citep{lanman2024chime}. However, we find using a sample of pulsar localizations that our astrometric accuracy is insensitive to this choice, and that two other reasonable calibrator selection strategies yield a similar astrometric accuracy (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

After a calibrator is selected, we apply delay and phase-calibration to each FRB in the sample to produce calibrated visibilities. For the majority of bursts in the sample, we were able to robustly measure delays in both polarizations. In the majority of cases the delays were coincident between the XX and YY polarization combinations to within 2 nanoseconds. In this scenario we average the delays between the XX and YY polarizations. In other scenarios the delays disagreed by more than 2 nanoseconds. In every scenario where this occurred, the burst was much more strongly detected in one polarization than the other, where the detection was marginal. We attribute this to the intrinsic polarization angle of the burst and the instrumental sensitivity, particularly at the KKO station. In this scenario we use the delay measured in the brighter of the two polarization hands only (XX or YY). The statistical uncertainty on each delay measurement was much smaller than the systematic uncertainties.

Finally, we use a maximum-likelihood delay-mapping procedure to translate these residual time delays into localization contours (See Eq. 4 in~\citet{marcote2020repeating}; also Eq. A1 in~\citet{leung2024vlbi}). Each VLBI localization contour is combined with the baseband localization ellipse as a prior. The combined contour is convolved with a kernel reflecting a conservative estimate of the localization accuracy. Finally, we calculate ellipse parameters (major/minor axes, orientation angle, and central coordinates) from the posterior probability grid.

To characterize the localization accuracy, we apply this procedure to a sample of $\approx 100$ pulsars. In doing so, we find that CHIME-KKO observations achieve an astrometric accuracy of $< 1''$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}), except at low declinations where additional RFI at the KKO site increases the overall RMS error. We conservatively prescribe the minor-axis astrometric uncertainty of the whole sample to 2'' at the $1\sigma$ level estimate the astrometric accuracy of the localizations, and find later on (see \S~\ref{sec:sample}) that our sample of robust host galaxy associations is unaffected by this choice.

\section{Host Association}\label{sec:association}
After localizing each burst with VLBI, we use the default priors in the PATH toolkit to assign posterior probabilities \pox\ to each catalogued galaxy allowed by the combined localization region from baseband and CHIME-KKO VLBI. Each localization region is cross-matched with a widefield galaxy survey. Where possible (55/81 fields), we utilize the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS) to find host galaxy candidates
due to its greater depth. In the remainder of observations (26/81 fields), we default to Pan-STARRS PS1, which covers the entire CHIME/FRB discovery footprint.

Stars are first identified in each field on the basis of their classification in Gaia DR3 and additional 
survey-specific criteria. For DECaLS, we required
$r > 14$ and that
DECaL\_type not be ``PSF''.
For PS1, we also use the
Probabilistic Classifications of Unresolved Point Sources in PanSTARRS1 catalog 
\citep[PS1-PSC;][]{tachibana2018} and the following
criteria on several catalog values:

\begin{itemize}
    \item rKronRad $ > 0$
    \item $r > 15$ 
    \item $\log_{10} | {\rm rPSFLikelihood} | < -2$
    \item  $\rm iPSFMag - iKronMag\_1 > 0.05$
\end{itemize}
\noindent


After the automated removal of these stars, each galaxy 
candidate in the field is assigned a probability $P(O|x)$ of being the true host of the FRB using 
the Probabilistically Associating Transients to their Hosts (PATH) framework~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic}. Finally, each field is visually inspected by at least one team member to identify potential misclassified and/or unaccounted stars and galaxies. When one of these issues is detected, it is manually corrected and PATH is run again for that field. 

%Accurately assigning these posterior probabilities is difficult when combining shallow, widefield archival imaging with a highly sensitive FRB detector such as CHIME. 
It is important to note that a highly sensitive 
FRB detector such as CHIME is likely to discover
FRBs with hosts that are too faint for the wide-field, archival imaging
we have used.
%In this regime, a significant fraction of bursts originates from beyond the maximum redshift out to which a faint FRB host would be detectable. 
As a point of reference, the faint dwarf host of FRB 20121102A~\citep{tendulkar2017host} would be detectable in the DECaLS $r$-band out to $z \sim 0.2$, depending on the Galactic extinction along the line of sight, implying an extragalactic DM of $\sim 200$ pc cm$^{-3}$. The redshift implied by the median DM of our sample significantly exceeds this (Fig.~\ref{fig:hg_vs_dm}).

The PATH framework accounts for the survey depth mismatch by specifying the ``unseen'' probability $P(U)$, which uses the FRB redshift distribution, the galaxy luminosity function, the localization ellipse footprint, and the optical survey depth to calculate the prior probability that the host galaxy of an individual FRB in a survey is too faint to be associated with a galaxy with high confidence. We choose values of $P(U)$ for the specific combination of FRBs detected by CHIME on the basis of a detailed injection test
analysis by~\citet{andersen2024inprep}
where mock FRBs are seeded in host galaxies as faint as $m_r = 28$ identified in the Hyper Suprime-Cam Subaru Strategic Survey and subsequently ``re-detected'' in the shallower optical surveys 
used for our FRB host associations. 
Their so-called ``sandbox'' analysis
provides a characterization of the fraction
of Type-I errors (i.e. falsely claiming a galaxy association where there is none). 
%Finally, a value of $P(U)$ is set fand a threshold value of \pox\ are chosen which minimizes the false positive fraction of associations in the sandbox. 

These simulations indicate that the optimal values of $P(U)$ are $0.32$ for the PanSTARRS fields, and $P(U) = 0.15$ for the DECaLS fields. We note that these values are higher than those previously used in 
the literature, e.g.\ $P(U) = 0.1$ is used for Pan-STARRS for the DSA-110 host galaxy sample~\citep{law2024deep}. %and $P(U) = 0.0$ are used in ASKAP-ICS follow-up fields~\citep{shannon2024commensal}. 
We defer a more extensive justification of these choices to~\citet{andersen2024inprep}, but note that this choice is likely conservative because the sample of bursts detectable by the outrigger stations presented here represent the brightest bursts detected by CHIME, and hence preferentially sample the nearby end of the CHIME redshift distribution~\citep{shin2023inferring}.

\section{Spectroscopic Followup}
We conducted spectroscopic follow-up observations for host galaxies in our sample with the primary goal of measuring their redshifts. We restricted follow-up observations to cases where the host association was considered secure, formally those with 
$P(O|x) > 0.9$; 19 of our 81 sightlines passed this criteria. 
Applying a Galactic extinction cut of $E(B-V) < 0.30$ reduced this to 13 sightlines. We also observed the host galaxy of FRB 20230730A even though it did not strictly pass the extinction criteria ($E(B-V) \sim 0.54$). In cases where the secondary galaxy was comparable in brightness, we overlapped the long-slit with both top host galaxy candidates. For 5 host galaxies, we used DEIMOS on the Keck II telescope in Mauna Kea, and for one host galaxy we used the Gemini North telescope in Mauna Kea as part of our Long and Large Program (PI: Eftekhari). For the other 9 host galaxies, we used the Kast Double Spectrograph mounted on the Shane 3-m telescope at Lick Observatory on Mount Hamilton in San Jose, California.

Two or more emission lines were used to confirm each redshift (see Table \ref{z_table}), except for the host galaxy of FRB 20230730A for which only one line was detectable. We attribute this to the high Galactic extinction along 
this sightline. 

\subsection{Ambiguous Associations}
Of all the potential targets, nine systems were borderline, in the sense that the two top host galaxy candidates satisfied  $P(\textrm{primary}|x) + P(\textrm{second}|x) > 90\%$. Of these nine systems, the secondary magnitude corresponded to 19.6-23.6. Confusion prevents us from securely associating faint host galaxies, so we secured redshifts for two of the three brightest secondary candidates. The brightest secondary candidates belonged to FRB 20230410A ($m_r = 19.60$; $z_{spec} = 0.5713$)
FRB 20231020A ($m_r = 19.9$), and FRB 20230311A ($m_r = 20.2$; $z_{spec} = 0.1924$). In the first ambiguous case, the high extragalactic DM (587 pc cm$^{-3}$) of the FRB prevents us from ruling out the secondary candidate, 
while in the final case the similar $z_{spec}$ of the primary and secondary candidate suggests a physical link between these two systems and suggests a robust association with the combined system.

\section{Multi-wavelength counterpart cross-matching}
The improved localizations for our sample of bright FRBs is a natural dataset in which to search for multi-wavelength counterparts to FRBs, due to the improved localization, large sample size (e.g.. 83 burst), and low redshifts of the bursts in the sample. Motivated by the discovery of compact radio counterparts \citep{chatterjee2017direct,niu2021repeating} and the association of some FRBs with magnetars, we cross-match the localization ellipses with the locations of known gamma-ray bursts, and search for compact persistent radio sources for the bursts within our sample. 

\subsection{High-energy counterpart search}

We search for temporal and spatial cross-matches between our 81 FRBs and 2589 long and short GRBs  triggers from the GRBWeb database\footnote{\url{https://user-web.icecube.wisc.edu/\~grbweb_public/}} and the General Coordinates Network\footnote{\url{gcn.nasa.gov}} between 1991 April 21 and 9 September 2024. Using only the GRBs with 1$\sigma$ localizations $<1^\circ$, we do not find any joint spatio-temporal (within 1 week in time and within 3$\sigma$ in spaces) coincidences. We do find three solely spatial cross-matches between FRBs 20231019A, 20230802A, 20230703A and three GRBs: 970306A, 120323A and 920221A*, respectively. Following the method presented in \citet{Curtin2024}, we simulate 1000 GRBs and cross-match each with the FRB sample to estimate the chance coincidence probability of three cross-matches. Each mock sample of GRBs has at least three cross matches, implying a high chance coincidence probability, which is consistent with the large uncertainty regions for these GRBs ($\gtrsim0.5^\circ$).  

\subsection{Persistent Radio cross-matching methodology}
Thus far, two FRBs have been confidently associated with persistent radio sources (PRS) \citep{chatterjee2017direct, niu2021repeating}. A third PRS was found to be associated with the active repeating FRB\,20201124A \citep{Bruni2024Nature}, but the larger spatial offset and physical size of this PRS makes it different from that of FRB 20121102A and 20190520B, which have been shown to be compact on milliarcsecond scales~\citep{marcote2017repeating,bhandariprs190520}.
We perform a systematic search for unresolved radio sources coincident with the CHIME-KKO FRBs in the Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty cm (FIRST; \citealt{FIRST}) and the NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS; \citealt{NVSS}) at 1.4\,GHz, as well as the Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS; \citealt{VLASS}) at 3\,GHz. For sources detected in either VLASS or FIRST, we also search the positions in the LOFAR Two-metre Sky Survey (LoTSS) Data Release 2 (DR2; \citealt{LoTSS}) at 144\,MHz. In each survey we searched for radio point sources above its $5\sigma$ detection threshold, using the Canadian Initiative for Radio Astronomy Data Analysis (CIRADA)\footnote{\url{https://cirada.ca/}} portal to access the image cutouts. 

In the FIRST and NVSS cutouts we estimated the noise by computing the RMS flux (i.e. $\sigma$) over the entire 2' $\times$ 2' cutout.  While this can lead to slightly overestimated noise estimates, if a particularly dominant radio source is present within the field, we found that this method was sufficient for achieving relatively uniform noise estimates across all fields. In VLASS, cutouts of the imaging residuals allowed for a better noise RMS estimate, which improves the sensitivity of our search and deepens our upper limits on persistent radio emission.

We summarize the results in Table \ref{tab:hostderived}. Given that the majority of the CHIME-KKO localizations fell outside of the FIRST footprint, we do not include limits from the survey in Table \ref{tab:hostderived} and instead discuss a few sources of interest in \S~\ref{sec:sample}. Likewise, the degraded sensitivity and angular resolution of NVSS make their limits less constraining, so we omit them from our calculations. While all fields targeted by VLASS yielded non-detections at the $5\sigma$ level, we identified three potential PRSs just below the detection threshold (but more than $3\sigma$) and discuss each of these sources in \S \ref{subsec:FRB20231206A}, \ref{subsec:FRB20231128A} and \ref{subsec:FRB20230926A}. \par

For the 14 FRBs with redshifts, we translate the VLASS RMS flux sensitivity into upper limits on the PRS luminosity at the redshift of the FRB at 3\,GHz, as listed in Table \ref{tab:hostderived} (excluding FRB\,20231204A, which had no associated VLASS query). Due to the low redshift of our host galaxy sample, the majority of our limits fall at or below the luminosities of PRSs associated with FRB\,20121102A and FRB\,20190520B \citep{chatterjee2017direct, niu2021repeating}, adding to the preponderance of evidences that those two sources are rare outliers in the FRB population \citep{Law_2022, law2024deep, sharma2024preferential}. If such fainter PRSs exist, our non-detections could be explained by lower sensitivities \citep[e.g.][]{Ibik2024prs}. Alternatively, the large angular size of the VLASS radio sources suggests that the radio emission may be partly contributed by ongoing star formation or an active galactic nucleus within the host galaxy \citep{Condon92, Condon19}.

Moreover, the potential PRS limits are consistent with those found for a sample of FRB repeaters \citep{Ibik2024prs}, and may suggest intrinsically fainter PRSs when compared to the PRSs associated with FRB\,20121102A and FRB\,20190520B, but similar to the PRS associated with FRB\,20201124A \citep{Bruni2024Nature}. These luminosity limits are also similar to those of the PRS analogs presented in \cite{Dong2024radiosources}. In the neutron star nebulae models (see e.g., \citealt{Kashiyama2017, margalit2018concordance}), the lower luminosities can be explained by factors such as older age, larger nebula radius, or intrinsically lower energy output \citep{Margalit2019nebulae}.

\section{Sources of Interest}\label{sec:sample}
In this section, we highlight some of the interesting sources in our sample. Both repeating FRBs in our sample have \textbf{potential} persistent radio source (PRS) candidates, but only one has a low chance coincidence probability. Three of our host galaxies are embedded in larger-scale structures: one repeating source is found in a starbursting galaxy merger, one in a massive X-ray cluster, and another in a rich optical cluster, and we comment on the rich potential of FRBs to probe these systems.

\subsection{FRB 20231204A: a repeating FRB probing the starburst gas in a galaxy merger}
FRB 20231204A is a burst from a repeating source which also produced FRB 20190303A, which~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} previously localized to an interacting galaxy pair. In that work, the localization was too large to identify the member galaxy. In this work, the updated localization provided by FRB 20231204A indicates that the FRB source likely originates in the member galaxy to the southwest. Briefly, long-slit spectroscopy and Baldwin-Phillips-Terlevich (BPT) diagram classification revealed both galaxies in the interacting system to be star-forming, $\sim L^*$ galaxies; We refer the interested reader to~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} for further details of its spectroscopic characterization.

As pointed out in~\citet{michilli2023subarcminute} it is possible to use this system to constrain the gas dynamics in a closely-interacting galaxy system, whose projected separation is $\sim$ 20 kiloparsecs. With a more precise localization, it will be possible to do so in great detail; however, the current localization accuracy restricts our analysis to an order-of-magnitude estimate. It also requires us to assume that the starburst gas does not ``move'' between the formation of its progenitor and the emission of the burst. Assuming that the delay time between the starburst and the emission of the FRB is much smaller than the dynamical timescale of the galaxy merger ($\sim  10^8$ year), the electron column traversed by the FRB can be used to probe the gas in the starburst. 

Hydrodynamical simulations have suggested that starbursts are fueled by inflows of metal-poor gas from the CGM when the gas loses angular momentum during a galaxy encounter. However, when galaxies in a two-body encounter pass significantly closer than their virial radii as in the case of FRB 20231204A, the ISM of the constituent galaxy disks gets disturbed, and in these scenarios it has been proposed that gas in the ISM (rather than the CGM) triggers star formation~\citep{sparre2023gas}. 
This picture is consistent with a rough estimate of the ionized gas content of the host galaxy ISM.~\citet{tempel2014flux} has estimated the luminosity of the merging galaxies to be $2 \times 10^{11} L_\odot$: ten times greater than that of the Milky Way, whose HI surface density exceeds $\Sigma \gtrsim 10^{21}$ cm$^{-3}$ out to $\sim 20$ kiloparsecs~\citep{kalberla2009hi}. We use this as a lower limit on the HI surface density of the gas involved in the starburst, and combine it with the DM budget of the sightline of FRB 20231204A, to constrain the ionization state $f_\mathrm{ion}$ of the star-forming gas. 
The rest-frame DM excess of 112 pc cm$^{-3}$ constrains the column-averaged ionization fraction to be $\lesssim 34\%$: consistent with a gas column from an ISM, where the ionization fraction is expected to be $\sim 10\%$ (e.g.~\citet{he2013correlation}):
$$\left(\dfrac{f_{ion}}{0.34}\right)\left(\dfrac{\Sigma}{10^{21}\textrm{ cm}^{-3}}\right) \lesssim 112\textrm{ pc cm}^{-3}.$$

FRB 20231204A is reminiscent of some other FRB hosts in the literature which show evidence of dynamical  disturbances~\citep{michalowski2021asymmetric,kaur2022fast}. However, those bursts do not have atypical values of DM$_\textrm{host}$ and the merger activity in those scenarios is not as dramatic as in the case of FRB 20231204A. In the future, a sample of precisely-localized fast radio bursts as well as auxiliary observations characterizing the neutral gas of the star formation site ~\citep[e.g., ][]{leewadell2023host,yamanaka2024alma,glowacki2023wallaby} may be able to discriminate between the ISM and CGM contributions to star formation during galaxy mergers, as well as the delay-time distribution of FRBs.

\subsection{FRB 20231206A: an FRB behind Abell 576 with an associated persistent radio source candidate}\label{subsec:FRB20231206A}
The luminous host galaxy and large dispersion measure excess of FRB 20231206A (295 pc cm$^{-3}$) led us to associate its host galaxy to Abell 576~\citep{abell1989catalog}. This X-ray cluster at $z \approx 0.03$ occupies $\sim 9$ square degrees on the sky and hosts over a thousand galaxies. A subset of the galaxies comprising the cluster comprise a high-velocity tail~\citep{rines2001infall} which has led to its identification as a merger of two subcomponents with a relative velocity of $\sim 9000$ km/s~\citep{dupke2007merger}. The host galaxy of FRB 20231206A | one of the most luminous in our sample | is part of the infalling sub-cluster. It has been proposed on the basis of the X-ray morphology of the cluster core that the sub-structure has already made its first passage through the core~\citep{kempner2004chandra} but neither the morphological nor the redshift information can reveal whether the sub-structure is in front or behind the cluster as viewed from earth.

The DM excess of FRB 20231206A can break this degeneracy. At the distance of the cluster center, the host galaxy of FRB 20231206A is transversely displaced by $\approx 5$ Mpc from the cluster center, whose extent is quantified by its $r_{200} = 2.02$ Mpc. At the virial radius the mass of the cluster is between $1-1.4 \times 10^{15} M_\odot$ ~\citep{rines2000infall,girardi1998optical}. We argue that the large (390 pc cm$^{-3}$) dispersion measure excess is more consistent with a geometry where the FRB host lies behind the X-ray cluster. In a scenario where the intracluster medium is completely ionized and where the FRB host lies behind the cluster's dark matter halo, the observed dispersion measure excess then constrains the ionized gas column of the ICM at large impact parameters (in this case, a gas density measurement at 2 $R_{200}$) far beyond the typical reach of X-ray based gas fraction measurements which typically the cluster gas fraction at $\sim R_{2500}-R_{500}$ ~\citep[e.g.,][]{ettori1999rosat, mantz2022cosmological}.

\section{A direct measurement of the host contribution to the DM budget}
The distribution of DM$_\textrm{h}$ is crucial to virtually all attempts to forward model FRB dispersion measures as probes of ionized gas. For instance, FRBs have been touted as excellent probes of the baryon density $\Omega_b$, IGM gas properties, and even the Hubble constant; yet these quantities are often degenerate with host galaxy properties in models ~\citep[e.g. Figure 9 of][]{james2022measurement}. FRBs have also been touted as probes of astrophysical feedback through one-point and two-point cross-correlation analyses~\citep{rafiei_ravandi2021chime,2019arXiv190102418M}, yet cross-correlation forecasts are sensitive to the noise added by host galaxy contamination of DM measurements.

Individual DM budgets have been observed to undershoot ~\citep[FRB 20220319B; see][]{ravi2023deep} and overshoot the total due to an insufficiently-modeled sightline ~\citep[e.g. FRB 20190520A][]{niu2021repeating,lee2023frb}. With every new FRB, observers are motivated to dissect in great detail~\citep{2024arXiv240200505K} the origin of the DM excess after subtracting DM$_\text{cosmic}$: is the remainder dominated by a dense circum-source medium, or could it possibly originate from gas along the line of sight?

At increasing redshift this question is difficult to answer because the contribution of large-scale structure to the DM budget -- which overdensities/underdensities relative to $\langle \textrm{DM}_\text{cosmic}\rangle$ -- dominates over smaller-scale contributions from e.g. the host galaxy and circum-source medium. At lower redshifts this question is more tractable since the mean cosmic contribution 
%and the large-scale structure contributions 
to the DM budget is minimized, and the discrete structures responsible for DM excesses and deficits may be identifiable with little or no casework.

This is illustrated qualitatively by the empirically-observed distribution of redshifts and DMs.  We have compiled the vast majority of FRBs with secure redshifts and publicly-available photometry detected to date, using the host galaxies in this paper to assemble a sample of 49 host galaxies at $z < 0.2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:macquart}). This allows us to make the most precise measurement of the distribution of the  residual extragalactic DMs, which we refer to as \dmh.

\begin{equation}
\dfrac{\dmh}{1 + z_h} = \textrm{DM}_\mathrm{tot} - \langle \textrm{DM}_\textrm{cosmic}(z_h)\rangle - \textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,ISM}} - \textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,halo}}.
\label{eq:dmh_definition}
\end{equation}
with $z_h$ the redshift of the host galaxy and where the Milky Way disk and halo contributions  $\textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,ISM}}$ and $\textrm{DM}_{\textrm{MW,halo}}$ are estimated using the NE2001~\citep{cordes2002ne2001} and~\citet{yamasaki2020galactic} models respectively.
In principle, \dmh\ includes contributions from line-of-sight structures, but at low redshifts it is likely to be dominated by contributions from the host galaxies of FRBs. It is often parameterized by modeling it probilistically as a log-normal distribution with a mean $\muhost$ and width $\sighost$. This parametrization lacks quantitative justification, yet is ubiquitous \citep[e.g.~][]{macquart2020census,james2021z,baptista2024measuring}.

To improve on this previous work, in the last section of this paper we combine our low-redshift host sample with FRBs in the literature to characterize the $\dmh$ distribution. Rather than fitting a log-normal distribution we use a simpler non-parametric method, where we measure the moments (i.e. the mean, width, skew, and excess kurtosis) of the $\dmh$ distribution. Using the moments of the $\dmh$ distribution captures crucial information that the log-normal distribution may not, since it allows the RMS scatter (the variance) in the distribution to be characterized independently of its heavy tail, i.e. its skew. Effects such as scattering and propagation through the inclined disks of their host galaxies will modify the moments of the $\dmh$; in the future the locations of FRBs within their host galaxies could potentially be disentangled by their distinct imprints on the moments of the $\dmh$ distribution.

We first summarize all available data on the $\dmh$ distribution in Figs.~\ref{fig:macquart} and~\ref{fig:host_dm}. Next, we identify a few critical subsamples of FRBs:
\begin{itemize} 
\item We exclude dwarf hosts \citep[FRB 20121102A, FRB 20190520B, and FRB 20210117A; ][]{bhandari2023nonrepeating} which represent outliers from the population of detected FRBs and are omitted from the remainder of this analysis. For this reason, our \dmh\ parameter estimates are valid only for FRBs within a certain range of host stellar masses which excludes dwarf galaxies($-23 \lesssim M_r < -17$).
\item Low-DM FRBs, for which the low total DM was crucial to making a host galaxy associations ~\citep[8 bursts from ][]{bhardwaj2024host,michilli2023subarcminute,Ibik2024host}. Analyzing this sub-sample separately confirms their anomalous \dmh\ properties.
\item Group/galaxy cluster FRBs: FRB 20220914A and FRB 20220509G~\citep{connor2023deep}, FRB 20231206A, FRB 20230203A, and FRB 20231204A in a galaxy merger (see \S.~\ref{sec:sample}). While we have not performed a complete search, we note that clusters account for a significant fraction of the largest outliers ($\dmh > 200$ pc cm$^{-3}$) observed in our sample, suggesting that we are complete to associated clusters whose foreground DM contribution exceeds this threshold.
\item Edge-on host galaxies, for which we report inclinations where available. These include FRB 20210603A \citep[$\iota \sim 83^\circ$; ][]{cassanelli2022fast}, FRB 20220207C \citep[$\iota \sim 74-80^\circ$;][]{law2024deep,bhardwaj2024selection}, and two FRBs from this work: FRB 20230203A ($\iota \sim 77-81^\circ$) and FRB 20231005A ($\iota \sim 81^\circ$), for which we apply the InclinationZoo estimator to publicly-available SDSS and Pan-STARRS imaging, verifying that that measurements are robust between both images. Three remaining FRBs have no quantitative estimates readily available, but appear highly elongated in published images justifying their classification as edge-on: FRB 20231120A~\citep{2024arXiv240916964S}, and FRB 20240201A and FRB 20240310A from~\citet{shannon2024commensal}. 
\end{itemize}

We then measure the first four moments of the $\dmh$ distribution, weighting each data point to account for errors in the NE2001 model and an RMS cosmic contribution, and de-biasing the kurtosis estimate such that a Gaussian distribution of \dmh\ values has $\kappa_h = 0$. %$\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$ to the results of dispersion-measure ray tracing through the Illustris TNG simulation~\citep{walker2024dispersion}, but note that the small redshift range ($z < 0.2$) prevents us from disentangling our fitted parameter $S_h^2$ from $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}^2$, which adds 63.3 pc cm$^{-3}$ of RMS scatter at $z = 0.1$ and 105 pc cm$^{-3}$ at $z = 0.2$. 
.

$$\langle \dmh\rangle = \dfrac{\sum_i w_i \dmh{}_{,i}}{\sum_i w_i} $$
$$S_h = \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh{}_{,i} - M_h)^2}{\sum_i w_i} $$
$$\Gamma_h = \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh{}_{,i} - M_h)^3}{S_h^{3}\sum_i w_i} $$
$$\kappa_h = \dfrac{\sum_i w_i (\dmh{}_{,i} - M_h)^4}{S_h^{4}\sum_i w_i} - 3.$$

The weights are defined to be $w_i \propto \sigma_i^{-2}$ with $$\sigma_i^2 = (0.2\text{DM}_\textrm{NE2001,i})^2 + (10~\text{pc cm}^{-3})^2 + \sigma_\textrm{cosmic}^{2}.$$ This formula accounts for 20\% uncertainties in the Milky Way disk model, a constant uncertainty in the MW halo model, and an RMS scatter in the cosmic dispersion measure originating from haloes as well as the IGM. We fix $\sigma_\textrm{cosmic} = 80~\text{pc cm}^{-3}$ in order to be roughly consistent with estimates from~\citet{walker2024dispersion}. While it is difficult to separate the RMS scatter in the IGM from $S_h$ we find that our results are insensitive to the particular value of $\sigma_\textrm{IGM}$ used, since it only affects the relative weighting of the data. Choosing $\sigma_\textrm{IGM} = 0$ shifts the final parameter estimates by at most $5\%$ of our quoted measurement uncertainty, which was estimated by bootstrapping each sample with replacement 4000 times. 

Our final parameter estimates for various sub-samples are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:mvsk} and Table~\ref{tab:mvsk}. They are the most precise ($\sim 10\%$) estimates to date and are derived without reference to uncertain models for galactic feedback or any particular functional form of the distribution, which is sensitive to the structure of the host galaxy.
We put them in the context of existing measurements of $\mu_\textrm{host}$ and $\sigma_\textrm{host}$ \citep{james2021z,shin2023inferring,connor2024gas} using analytical expressions for the mean, variance, skew, and kurtosis of the log-normal distribution.

First, we split our sample based on the median $M_r$. The $\langle\dmh \rangle$ distribution for host galaxies within the ranges $-23 > M_r > -20.6$ and $-20.6 > M_r > -17$ differ by $\approx 30$ pc cm$^{-3}$. While current statistics imply that this is only a 1$\sigma$ difference, it is a precautionary tale to attempts to simplistically model the host galaxy contribution.

Second, we find that relative to the parent sample (``All''), FRBs in groups and clusters and edge-on host galaxies have much higher values of $\langle \dmh\rangle$ ($> 100$ pc cm$^{-3}$) but similar values of $S_h$ to that of the parent sample. This implies that these systems will bias cosmological analyses which are sensitive to $\langle\dmh\rangle$.

Third, we find that low-DM selected FRBs have an extremely skewed \dmh\ distribution, implying they should be omitted from population-level cosmological analyses entirely.

Finally, we find that the skew of the \dmh\ distribution is positive, an indication of high-DM tail of FRBs.

We also sub-divide the clean sample by detection instrument to check for radio-related biases. Even though~\citet{james2021z} does not strictly consist of ASKAP/ICS sources, we see consistency between our ASKAP/ICS-only sample and the aforementioned global fit, which is dominated by ASKAP/ICS sightlines. Similarly, we see consistency at the 1$\sigma$ level between~\citet{connor2024gas} and our assembled DSA-110 sub-sample, with the caveat that not many sightlines are available. Finally, the CHIME/FRB only sample dramatically improves the constraining power relative to~\citet{shin2023inferring}. All three sub-samples are consistent at the 1$\sigma$ level.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
The challenge of localizing FRBs and associating them to their host galaxies has been a bottleneck in the ongoing effort to understand their origins. Wide-field telescopes allow for FRB discovery, yet these instruments do not have sufficient angular resolution. In this paper we have demonstrated  the viability of wide-field VLBI on FRBs on a large sample of bursts, using data from the first operational CHIME outrigger KKO~\citep{lanman2024chime}. We use a VLBI observation strategy and a calibrator grid developed in prior papers~\citep{leung2021synoptic,andrew2024vlbi} to present arcsecond-scale localizations for 81 sources, secure host galaxy associations for 19, and new spectroscopic redshifts for 14, many of which are at low redshift $(z < 0.2)$. 

These low-redshift galaxies are rich targets for understanding the origin of FRBs: both repeating sources in our sample are associated with potential persistent radio source candidates in archival data. One source, FRB 20240209A, displays a large offset from its massive elliptical host; see papers in prep by Shah et al. (2024) and Eftekhari et al. (2024). Towards other sightlines, upper limits on persistent radio counterparts challenge the existence of PRS at or below the luminosity of known PRS. In addition, at low redshifts, the imprint of the IGM is minimized. This allows us to identify sightlines with high excess dispersion, which in many cases can be attributed to nearby galaxy clusters, or edge-on host galaxy. We use this additional statistical power to directly measure the enigmatic host galaxy contribution to FRB dispersion measures in a way that is agnostic to any particular parameterization of feedback (e.g. the $F$ parameter) and fluctuations in the IGM. It represents a significant advancement over global fitting methods which must use a combination of sources at high and low redshifts to break degeneracies. Model-independent measurements of $\dmh$ like the one here will be a crucial anchor for FRB cosmology at higher redshifts, and contain rich information about the spatial distribution of FRBs within their hosts. More broadly, the observational techniques for FRB detection and localization, host galaxy association and follow-up pave the way for upcoming large-scale FRB surveys~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian,hallinan2019dsa}, which will revolutionize our understanding of FRBs and the universe they traverse.

\section{Astrometric Calibration}\label{sec:accuracy}
\subsection{Baseband Localization: Accuracy of Quoted Declination Uncertainties}
We localize a total of $100$ single pulses from $18$ distinct, well-localized pulsars. The pulses cover a wide range of detected S/N values ($5 \leq \text{S/N} \leq 100$) and declinations ($+6^\circ \leq \delta \leq +80^\circ$). The Crab pulsar -- PSR B0531+21 -- as well one pulsar below a declination of $\delta < +6^\circ$ are omitted from the sample. Baseband localisations of the Crab are systematically offset by $\sim 2 ~\mathrm{arcmin}$ West of South of CHIME's primary beam. We suspect this offset arises from confusion with the surrounding, radio-bright nebula whose angular extent of roughly $5~\mathrm{arcmin}$ in declination at $5~\mathrm{GHz}$ \citep{Bietenholz_2004} makes it incompletely resolved by the short internal baselines of CHIME. We do not observe this behavior in any other pulsar at similar declinations to the Crab and therefore rule that the Crab is an outlier. We have also identified instances for which pulses originating from PSR B1642-03 are poorly baseband localised for reasons that are not yet entirely well-understood. We suspect that imperfect characterization of CHIME's response to variations in ambient temperature is likely the driving factor at these very-low declinations \citep{michilli2020analysis}. Unfortunately, the lack of bright, single-pulse emitting pulsars between $-3^\circ\leq \delta \leq +6^\circ$ limits our ability to robustly test baseband localisations within this region of CHIME's primary beam. The FRB sample presented in this paper contains one FRB that lies within this region. As a final cut, we limit the analysis to pulses detected within CHIME's primary beam that have a detection (``L4'') signal-to-noise ratio of $> 5$ (all FRBs presented in this paper sample obey these criteria).

For the remaining set of 100 pulses, we plot a summary of the baseband localizations performed to validate the uncertainties along the declination axis in Figure \ref{fig:bb_pulsars}. In the left panel, we plot the measured declination offset along with its associated uncertainty as a function of detection S/N for each pulse in black. We overplot the theoretical uncertainty for an FRB detected at $\nu = 600~\mathrm{MHz}$ at zenith by CHIME in red. This uncertainty increases as the angle from zenith increases ($\sigma_\theta\propto 1/\mathrm{cos}\theta$; \citep{michilli2020analysis}), hence why some values tend away from the theoretical prediction. For the 19 FRBs presented in this paper, we plot their measured S/N and associated decl. uncertainties in blue at a fiducial offset of $-2.5^\circ$ for visual comparison with pulsars in a similar range of S/N values. In the right plot, we plot a histogram of the declination offsets normalised by their uncertainties. Assuming that the baseband localisation pipeline is well-calibrated, we expect the distribution of values to be Gaussian distributed with mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1. We measure a mean of 0.1 and standard deviation of 1.0, suggesting that the data is well described by Gaussian statistics. We overplot the corresponding Gaussian distribution in red. The results of this analysis reinforces that the major axes of the CHIME-KKO localisations are well-calibrated over a wide range of detected S/N and declinations.

\subsection{VLBI Localization: Declination-dependent astrometric accuracy and impact of Calibrator Selection}
We perform a similar validation check of our VLBI localization procedure, using 62 single pulse localizations of 18 distinct pulsars at a variety of declinations (see Fig.~\ref{fig:psr_cal_test}). Here, we explore the impact of the often ad-hoc choice of VLBI calibrator on the resulting astrometry. For instance, selecting the closest calibrator minimizes the impact of ionospheric and beam variations, but choosing the calibrator which maximizes the S/N of the target after applying delay and phase solutions can ensure a more robust detection of the FRB. However, under either rule, it is possible to choose a calibrator that is impacted by an unknown systematic, such as those detected in a sidelobe or confused sources. Since we expect all sources to have a similar total instrumental delay, we can mitigate this risk by comparing the delays of all calibrators in the field, which we use to identify outliers. We thus adopt the following strategy for calibrator selection --- for all calibrators detected in the field, we select those whose measured total delays are within 2~ns of the median delay. From these, we choose the calibrator closest in declination to the baseband localization of the FRB.

To compare this with other strategies, we perform localizations using all detected VLBI calibrators for a set of pulsars with known positions, and compare the residuals between the measured and true positions. Figure~\ref{fig:psr_cal_test} shows the offsets for a set of between 1 and 5 observations of 18 pulsars. For a fixed x-coordinate on the plot, each column of points represents a single observation (i.e., baseband dump). Each point in the column represents the measured astrometric offset for a particular in-beam calibrator. The gray points are the calibrators which were not chosen, while the colored points indicate the offsets measured with the calibrator chosen using one of three selection criteria. -- ``Closest \& Good'' refers to the strategy described in the last paragraph, while ``S/N'' and ``Closest'' refer to using the calibrator which maximizes the S/N of the phase-referenced target visibilities, and the closest calibrator on the sky in declination, respectively.
Fig.~\ref{fig:psr_offsets} shows the offset distribution for pulsars localized as a function of which strategy is used. Their RMS values are quite similar, with a few outliers originating from pulsars at low declinations. 

We regard the associations in this paper as highly secure on the basis of the localization accuracy demonstrated in this appendix, in light of the $\sim10\%$ Type-I error rate for the adopted PATH threshold. In addition, we have inflated our astrometric error bars ($\approx 1.5''$ for all declinations, $\approx 0.93''$ excluding low declinations) to 2'' for the whole sample. We note that any individual host in the sample may be misidentified with the magnitude-dependent false-positive rate of $\sim 10\%$ as described in the text; the small number of low-declination systems (only FRB 20231123A and FRB 20230222A fall at declinations lower than $21^\circ$) also are slightly more subject to astrometric errors than the rest. The combined effect of astrometric errors, however, are most likely subdominant to the host association false-positive rate; the effect on population-level statistics of the full sample is likely to be minimal.
