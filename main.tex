\begin{abstract}
Out of 269 bright (S/N $> 20$) fast radio bursts detected by the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) between December 2022 and February 2024, we present 83 fast radio bursts localized to 2'' by $\sim 1'$ precision. Of these 83 bursts, we use the PATH algorithm to associate them to 18 new host galaxies, and spectroscopic redshifts for 14.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are short-duration radio pulses whose shocking diversity has earned them a reputation as perhaps the most mysterious class of astrophysical transients~\citep{cordes2019fast,petroff2022fast}. While thousands of FRBs have been discovered~\citep{collaboration2021chime} spanning several orders of magnitude in luminosity, rise time, burst duration, and activity timescale, their origins remain unknown and their potential as cosmological probes remains untapped -- bottlenecked by a lack of robust burst localization and host galaxy identification. 

The host galaxies of FRBs are central to the study of FRB progenitors and their eventual utility as cosmological probes. Following in the footsteps of the gamma-ray burst (GRB) community~\citep{bloom2002observed,fong2022comprehensive},
several early studies have been conducted on FRB host galaxies. With a handful of bursts (5), early studies of sources discovered by the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder~\citep{macquart2020census} found an offset distribution compatible with short GRB hosts and supernovae~\citep{heintz2020host,bhandari2020host}. This was expanded by ~\citet{bhandari2022characterizing} and~\citet{bhardwaj2024host} (15 and 18 hosts), and~\citet{law2024deep}, who found a broad diversity of delay times and formation channels using 11 new hosts of FRBs discovered and localized by the Deep Synoptic Array~\citep{kocz2019dsa}. 

These studies have all been limited due to the small sample sizes involved. However, we are entering an era of of precise FRB localizations and plentiful host galaxy associations: the Deep Synoptic Array has detected 11 FRBs and localized them to their host galaxies~\citep{law2024deep}, with redshifts ranging from  $z = 0.04$ to $0.62$.
The recent completion of the ASKAP Fast Transient incoherent-sum survey~\citep{shannon2024commensal} has added an optically-complete sample of 43 FRBs and 30 redshifts with redshifts ranging from $z = 0.023$ to $1.02$, many of which have been characterized in the sample of~\citet{gordon2023demographics} (see also~\citet{gordon2023fast}).

This paper expands on these groundbreaking efforts using the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME/FRB) and KKO, the first of its three VLBI outrigger stations. Using triggered VLBI, we localize 83 FRB sources with an accuracy of $\sim 1' \times 2''$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:localization}), serving as a proof-of-concept of the triggered VLBI technique. The abundance of VLBA and RFC astrometric calibrators at 600 MHz which we observe is encouraging for next-generation FRB telescopes which will rely on similar techniques to pinpoint FRBs: these include CHIME's successor CHORD~\citep{vanderlinde2019canadian}, as well as other compact arrays being built as HIRAX~\citep{newburgh2016hirax}, BURSTT~\citep{lin2022burstt}, and CASPA~\citep{luo2024fast}. We systematically investigate the impact of calibrator choice on astrometric accuracy using a sample of single-pulse localizations from pulsars, finding that on this short baseline, our astrometric accuracy is unaffected by our calibrator choice (Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

The high detection rate of the CHIME/FRB backend~\citep{collaboration2018chime} allows 83 bright FRBs to be successfully localized. Despite the moderate accuracy of the single-pulse localizations, 18 of these bursts can confidently be associated with bright host galaxies, as quantified by a conservative application of the Bayesian host association algorithm PATH~\citep{aggarwal2021probabilistic} (Sec~\ref{sec:association}) and a detailed simulation of the rate of false-positive associations~\citep{andersen2024inprep}.

Finally Sec.~\ref{sec:sample} we describe the 18 hosts in the sample, and highlight a few interesting cases therein. They have brightnesses of $16.4 < m_r < 22.7$ and are all at low redshifts ($z < 0.33$), and like the sample presented in~\citet{law2024deep}, are uniformly selected from the Pan-STARRS~\citep{chambers2016pan, magnier2020pan,waters2020pan, magnier2020pan2, magnier2020pan3, flewelling2020pan} and Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS)~\citep{dey2019overview} imaging surveys. These bright galaxies represent ideal, nearby targets for spatially-resolved followup studies (e.g.~\citep{mannings2021high,woodland2023environments}), where followup facilities can discover faint optical hosts such as dwarf galaxies, which have been known to host a handful of unusual FRBs~\citep{tendulkar2017host,niu2021repeating}. Low-redshift host galaxies are also crucial components of FRB cosmology, providing the tightest constraints on host-galaxy contributions to the total DM along the sightline of an FRB~\citep{james2021z,jahnsschindler2023how}.

\section{FRB Detection and Initial Localization}\label{sec:localization}
\subsection{Initial burst detection at CHIME}
Between December 9 2022 through Feb 10 2024, CHIME/FRB detected $269$ bright (S/N $> 20$) FRB candidates which were later confirmed by two human tsars inspecting their dynamic spectra. 
Following the detection and real-time localization, triggers were sent to dump the full field of view at CHIME and KKO  for $\sim 100$ milliseconds. During this time, baseband data were successfully dumped for the majority of these 269 candidates at CHIME. This enabled the interferometric localization using the CHIME core (``baseband localization'') on arcminute scales. In many but not all of these cases, data were also successfully captured at the KKO outrigger, which was in a commissioning phase until about June 2023. We use the baseband dumps at CHIME and KKO to localize these bursts to 2-arcsecond scale precision along a roughly east-west axis defined by the orientation of the baseline. The localization ellipses are thus highly elongated along a roughly east-west axis, whose orientation angle depends on the exact declination at which each burst was detected.

\subsection{Single-pulse localization}
Single-pulse VLBI localization represents a new frontier in VLBI astrometry. Due to sparse $uv$ coverage and the transience of the signal, it is critical to realistically assess the magnitude of typical localization uncertainties. We do this using pulsar localizations at a variety of declinations and hour angles (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}. We rely on the CHIME baseband localization in the declination direction to localize the source in the north-south direction, while adding the KKO station brings $\sim 2''$ angular resolution primarily along the east-west direction.

\subsection{Minor-axis localization using VLBI}
Along the east-west direction, the position is strongly constrained by VLBI localization. Following the basic strategy of~\citep{leung2021synoptic}, we use the full-array baseband dumps to search for bright ($\sim$ 1 Jansky) VLBI calibrators from the RFC and VLBA catalogs within the baseband dump, whose effective field of view on the sky is $\sim 200$ deg$^2$. At each station, we formed station beams towards each of the $\sim 200$ VLBI calibrators within the field of view. Following~\citep{andrew2024calibrator}, we placed a single correlator pointing in each station beam, and each pointing was correlated using the~\texttt{PyFX} correlator~\citep{leung2024vlbi}. Visibilities were saved if the calibrator was detected. We detected up to 15 VLBI calibrators per baseband dump this way. We inspected the uncalibrated delay toward each calibrator. Typically, the VLBI calibrator delays agree to within 2 ns of each other, indicating that we do not need direction-dependent calibration at this level of delay accuracy; calibrators which have wildly discrepant delays can usually be associated with misidentified calibrators~\citep{andrew2024vlbi}. To incorporate this effect in our sample selection we reject calibrators whose delays are not within 2 ns of the median calibrator delay.
After rejecting confused calibrators, we choose the calibrator which minimizes the target-calibrator separation. However, we find using a sample of pulsar localizations that our astrometric accuracy is insensitive to this choice, and that choosing the calibrator which maximizes the S/N of the referenced target scan yields a similar astrometric accuracy (see Appendix~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

\section{Host Association}\label{sec:association}
After localizing each burst with VLBI, we use the default priors in the PATH toolkit to assign posterior probabilities to each catalogued galaxy allowed by the combined localization region from baseband and CHIME-KKO VLBI. Each localization region is cross-matched with a widefield galaxy survey. Where possible (55/78 fields), we cross-match against the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS) to find a host galaxy due to its greater depth. In the remainder of fields (23/78), we default to Pan-STARRS PS1, which covers the entire CHIME/FRB discovery footprint. \textbf{Note: Waiting for last 5 fields from incomplete PATH run; update these numbers.}

Stars are identified in each field on the basis of their classification in Gaia DR3. After this automated removal, each field is manually inspected for contamination by misclassified stars \textbf{and other failures}. Finally, each galaxy candidate in the field is assigned a probability of being the true host. \textbf{Note: would like somebody to review this}

Accurately assigning these posterior probabilities is difficult when combining shallow, widefield archival imaging with a highly sensitive FRB detector such as CHIME. In this case, a significant fraction of bursts originates from beyond the maximum redshift out to which a faint host galaxy would be detectable. As a point of reference, the faint dwarf host of the first repeater (FRB 20121102A) would be detectable in the DECaLS $r$-band out to $z \sim 0.2$, depending on the Galactic extinction along the line of sight, implying an extragalactic DM of $\sim 200$. The redshift implied by the median DM of our sample significantly exceeds this (Fig.~\ref{fig:hg_vs_dm}).

The PATH framework accounts for the survey depth mismatch by specifying the ``unseen'' probability $P(U)$, which uses the FRB redshift distribution, the galaxy luminosity function, the localization ellipse size, and the optical survey depth to calculate the prior probability that the host galaxy of an individual FRB in a survey is too faint or too confused to be associated with high confidence. We choose values of $P(U)$ for the specific combination of FRBs detected by CHIME on the basis of detailed simulations, which allow us to simulate a population of FRBs from host galaxies beyond the detection horizon of the optical survey and choose $P(U)$ to minimize the fraction
of Type-I errors (i.e. falsely claiming a galaxy association where there is none)~\citep{andersen2024inprep}. 

These simulations indicate that the optimal values of $P(U)$ are $0.32$ for the PanSTARRS fields, and $P(U) = 0.15$ for the DECaLS fields. We defer a more extensive justification of these choices to~\citet{andersen2024inprep}, but note that this choice is likely conservative because the sample of bursts detectable by the outrigger stations presented here represent the brightest bursts detected by CHIME, and hence preferentially sample the nearby end of the CHIME redshift distribution~\citep{shin2023inferring}.

\section{Radio Counterpart Cross-matching}
Thus far, there are two unambiguous associations of a luminous compact persistent radio source (PRS) with FRBs\,20121102A and 20190520B \citep{chatterjee2017direct,marcote2017repeating,Niu190520b,Bhandari+23b}. More recently, a third PRS was found to be associated with the active repeating FRB\,20201124A. However, the larger offset and physical size of the radio source makes this association more tentative. Here, we perform a systematic search for a radio source coincident with the CHIME-KKO FRBs using the the Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty cm (FIRST; \citealt{FIRST}) and the NRAO VLA Sky Survey (NVSS; \citealt{NVSS}) at 1.5\,GHz, and the Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS; \citealt{VLASS}) at 3\,GHz, similar to the analysis in \citep{ibik2024inprep}. \textcolor{red}{VD: Didn't we also check LOFAR?}

To perform the search, we utilized The Canadian Initiative for Radio Astronomy Data Analysis (CIRADA)\footnote{https://cirada.ca/} to access the image cutouts. We overlaid the localization ellipses of the CHIME/KKO sample and used the root-mean-square (rms) FITS files to determine the significance levels. The detection threshold is set to be XX for a radio detection.   


We find non-detections the FRBs in our sample in all archival surveys except FRB\,20231128A and placed upper limits on the PRS luminosities at 3\,GHz using their spectroscopic redshifts. The values are reported in Table \ref{tab:hostderived}. We first assess the non-detections and exclude the possibility of PRSs brighter than 1.2 $\times$ 10$^{29}$ erg/s/Hz for the FRBs in this sample, comparable to the luminosity of the PRS associated with FRB\,20121102A. If these fainter PRSs exist, the non-detections may be due to a combination of the sensitivity limits and cadence of the observing instrument. \section{Astrometric Calibration}\label{sec:accuracy}
We localize a total of $100$ single pulses from $18$ distinct, well-localized pulsars. The pulses cover a wide range of detected S/N values ($5 \leq \text{S/N} \leq 100$) and declinations ($+6^\circ \leq \delta \leq +80^\circ$). The Crab pulsar -- PSR B0531+21 -- as well one pulsar below a declination of $\delta < +6^\circ$ are omitted from the sample. Baseband localisations of the Crab are systematically offset by $\sim 2 ~\mathrm{arcmin}$ West of South of CHIME's primary beam. We suspect this offset arises from confusion with the surrounding, radio-bright nebula whose angular extent of roughly $5~\mathrm{arcmin}$ in declination at $5~\mathrm{GHz}$ \citep{Bietenholz_2004} makes it incompletely resolved by the short internal baselines of CHIME. We do not observe this behavior in any other pulsar at similar declinations to the Crab and therefore rule that the Crab is an outlier. We have also identified instances for which pulses originating from PSR B1642-03 are poorly baseband localised for reasons that are not yet entirely well-understood. We suspect that imperfect characterization of CHIME's response to variations in ambient temperature is likely the driving factor at these very-low declinations \citep{michilli2020analysis}. Unfortunately, the lack of bright, single-pulse emitting pulsars between $-3^\circ\leq \delta \leq +6^\circ$ limits our ability to robustly test baseband localisations within this region of CHIME's primary beam. The FRB sample presented in this paper contains one FRB that lies within this region: however, we caution using the associated host to draw any significant conclusions until the systematic effects at low declinations are better understood.\textbf{Only one, maybe two, of our associated host galaxies falls in this range, and the robustness of the association does not change if we increase the declination error (check this). Alternatively, the host galaxy could be downgraded to silver.} As a final cut, we limit the analysis to pulses detected within CHIME's primary beam that have a detected S/N $> 5$. All FRBs presented in this sample obey these criteria. Efforts to extend this analysis to sidelobe detections are currently underway and will be presented in future works. 

For the remaining set of 100 pulses, we plot a summary of the baseband localizations performed to validate the uncertainties along the declination axis in Figure \ref{fig:bb_pulsars}. In the left panel, we plot the measured declination offset along with its associated uncertainty as a function of detection S/N for each pulse in black. We overplot the theoretical uncertainty for an FRB detected at $\nu = 600~\mathrm{MHz}$ at zenith by CHIME in red. This uncertainty increases as the angle from zenith increases ($\sigma_\theta\propto 1/\mathrm{cos}\theta$; \citep{michilli2020analysis}), hence why some values tend away from the theoretical prediction. For the 18 FRBs presented in this paper, we plot their measured S/N and associated decl. uncertainties in blue at a fiducial offset of $-2.5^\circ$ for visual comparison with pulsars in a similar range of S/N values. In the right plot, we plot a histogram of the declination offsets normalised by their uncertainties. Assuming that the baseband localisation pipeline is well-calibrated, we expect the distribution of values to be Gaussian distributed with mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1. We measure a mean of 0.1 and standard deviation of 1.0, suggesting that the data is well described by Gaussian statistics. We overplot the corresponding Gaussian distribution in red. The results of this analysis reinforces that the major axes of the CHIME-KKO localisations are well-calibrated over a wide range of detected S/N and declinations.

Selecting the closest calibrator minimizes the impact of ionospheric and beam variations, but choosing the brightest calibrator can ensure a more robust detection. In both strategies, we could accidentally choose a calibrator that is impacted by an unknown systematic, such as those detected in a sidelobe or confused sources. Since we expect all sources to be affected by similar total instrumental delay, we can mitigate this risk by comparing the delays of all calibrators in the field and identify outliers. We thus adopt the following strategy for calibrator selection --- for all calibrators detected in the field, select those whose measured total delays are within 2~ns of the median delay. From these, choose the calibrator closest in declination to the baseband localization of the FRB.

To compare this with other strategies, we perform localizations using all available calibrators for a set of known pulsars, and compare the offsets of the measured to true positions. Figure~\ref{fig:psr_cal_test} shows the offsets for a set of between 1 and 5 observations of 18 pulsars. Each column of points represents a single observation (i.e., baseband dump), with each point the result of localizing using a different in-beam calibrator. The colored points indicate the results of using the calibrator chosen with each selection criterion -- ``Closest \& Good'' refers to the strategy described in the last paragraph, while ``S/N'' and ``Closest'' refer to using the calibrator with the highest S/N cross-correlation and the closest calibrator in declination, respectively.

For almost all of the calibrators tested, our chosen selection method (green points) ensures offsets are less than 2.5 arcsec.
